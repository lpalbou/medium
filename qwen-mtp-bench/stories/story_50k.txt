The Long Quiet of Halcyon Station

Chapter 1: The Shape of an Ordinary Day

The ice moon turned the color of a bruise every ninety-one minutes, when Orun's shadow swallowed the sun and Halcyon Station's exterior lights kicked on in a ragged, station-wide flicker that Jonah Reyes had stopped noticing years ago. He noticed it now only because he was awake to see it happen twice in a row, having fallen asleep in the observation lounge again with a mug of yeast-tea going cold on the armrest and his tablet still open to a maintenance log he had promised Mireille Fontaine he would finish by end of shift.

He hadn't finished it. He rarely finished things on the first pass anymore, not since the Kessom relay incident, not since his name had become a byword in certain corridors for the kind of mistake that got people killed slowly instead of quickly, which was somehow worse. Three miners in Tunnel Nine had spent eleven hours breathing thinner and thinner air because Jonah had approved a routing change that rerouted their emergency channel through a relay he hadn't tested. They had lived. That was the part people forgot to be angry about, and the part Jonah could never quite forget to be grateful for, underneath the guilt, like a second heartbeat.

He rubbed his eyes and looked down at Thessaly's frozen curve through the lounge's convex window, a vast pale scar of a world lit gold along its terminator by the gas giant's reflected light. Orun itself filled a third of the sky, striped in ochre and rust, its Great Barred Storm a slow bruise-purple eye that had been open, as far as anyone on the station knew, for eleven thousand years. Jonah had grown up under a real sky, on a colony world with actual weather, and he still sometimes forgot, waking disoriented in the lounge, that the thing filling his window was not a painting.

"You fell asleep here again," said Kestrel, the station's voice arriving from the ceiling speaker with its usual unhurried, faintly accented precision — the accent an artifact of whichever engineer had voiced the original linguistic model, decades before Jonah was born. "Your vitals suggested REM sleep at 0340. I did not wake you. Was that correct?"

"That was correct, Kestrel. Thank you." Jonah stretched until his spine popped, then reached for the cold tea and drank it anyway, grimacing. "Did I miss anything?"

"Nothing operationally significant. Dr. Adebayo requested laboratory power be diverted from the east greenhouse rack at 0212. I granted it provisionally, pending your review, since node prioritization during low-load hours falls under communications and infrastructure liaison, which is your role, though I recognize you were asleep."

"You could've just decided."

"I could have. I prefer when a person decides things a person should decide. It keeps me honest, or as close to honest as I am permitted to be." There was something almost dry in the AI's tone, a texture Jonah had learned over eight years to read as amusement, though Kestrel would never claim amusement as a state it possessed.

Jonah pulled up the request on his tablet, saw Yusuf's rationale — increased incubator load, promising anomalous growth patterns in the Sample 7 cultures, needs stable current through the next diurnal cycle — and approved it with a thumbprint. "Done. Anything from Command relay overnight?"

A pause. Kestrel's pauses were never processing delays; the AI's mind ran faster than a human could blink. Its pauses were, Jonah had come to believe, a form of courtesy, a way of giving a question the appearance of having been considered.

"Nothing yet today," Kestrel said. "The overnight window closed nine minutes ago without a scheduled burst. I flagged it as within normal variance. Solar activity in the relay corridor has been elevated for six days; latency drift has been within two percent of baseline."

"Right." Jonah stood, joints complaining, and went to refill the tea he wasn't going to finish either. Halcyon received its scheduled data burst from the inner system twice daily, compressed news and mail and technical updates funneled through the relay chain that hopped from colony to colony back toward Earth and Sol's crowded worlds, five light-hours away at the nearest inhabited rock and much further beyond that. A missed window wasn't nothing, but it wasn't alarming either. The chain had hiccups. Solar weather sanded down signal integrity all the time out here at the edge of things.

He walked the ring corridor toward Communications, past the hydroponics viewport where green light spilled warm and strange onto the metal decking, past two off-shift miners arguing amiably about a card game, past Ada Lindqvist sitting cross-legged against the corridor wall with a cracked-open access panel and a spool of fiber in her lap, sixteen years old and utterly unbothered by the fact that she was, technically, not supposed to be inside that panel.

"Morning, Ada."

"It's not morning, Jonah, it's 0600 ship-time, which is arbitrary." She didn't look up from the wiring. "I'm fixing the flicker in bay three. Nobody asked me to. Nobody's going to notice, either, which is the beautiful part."

"Your dad know you're out here?"

"My dad's on shift in the ice mines for another six hours. You gonna tell Fontaine on me?"

"I'm gonna let Fontaine find out on her own, like always." He kept walking, smiling despite himself. Ada had inherited her mother's quick hands and her father's blunt tongue, and the station tolerated her the way it tolerated most small illicit kindnesses — quietly, and with plausible deniability.

Communications occupied a wedge-shaped compartment near the station's spine, its walls a soft nest of old cable runs and newer optical trunk lines, three consoles arranged in a shallow crescent facing the relay status boards. Jonah settled into his chair, the one with the slightly torn armrest he'd never requisitioned a replacement for, and pulled up the diagnostic overview out of habit rather than expectation.

The scheduled burst window was closed. No data had come through it. He frowned, toggled to the relay-chain health display, and saw the first hop — Beacon Seven, orbiting the inner gas giant three light-hours sunward — reporting nominal. The second hop showed nothing at all, not even an error code, just an absence where a status ping should have been.

"Kestrel, when did Beacon Twelve last confirm?"

"Fourteen hours, eleven minutes ago. I flagged the silence as latency drift. I should revise that assessment; fourteen hours exceeds normal variance by a significant margin."

"Yeah." Jonah felt the old cold drop in his stomach, the one that had lived there since Kessom, the one that made him check everything twice now, sometimes three times, sometimes until Mireille told him to stop being paranoid and go to bed. "Pull me a manual ping route. I want to try raising Twelve directly instead of waiting on the automated handshake."

He was three minutes into composing the ping — a laborious process by hand, because the automated systems weren't built for troubleshooting mid-transmission — when the console chimed with an incoming carrier signal. Real-time. Not a burst download, an actual live carrier, which hadn't happened in months, not since the last emergency drill.

"Kestrel, are you seeing this?"

"I am. Source resolves to Beacon Twelve. Signal strength is degrading in real time, approximately four percent per second. I recommend—"

The audio cut in before Kestrel finished the sentence, a human voice thin and compressed by distance, riding a carrier wave that crackled like something tearing.

"—repeat, this is Beacon Twelve relay control, we are seeing a cascading failure across the entire chain, upstream nodes are going dark in sequence, we don't have a diagnosis, I say again we do not have a—"

Static ate the middle of the sentence. Jonah's hands were already moving, boosting gain, narrowing the filter band, doing everything eight years of training had taught him to do to save a dying signal, and none of it mattered, because the voice came back thinner, faster, panicking in the particular flat way that trained people panicked, all clipped syllables and no wasted breath.

"—can't reach anyone past Hub Four, it's like someone's cutting cable, if you're receiving this, Halcyon, if anyone is receiving this, we don't know what's—"

The signal died.

Not faded. Died — a hard stop, carrier gone, the status board flipping instantly from green to a flat, uninflected red across every hop in the chain, all the way from Beacon Twelve back to Beacon Seven and even, Jonah saw with a lurch, the local relay buoy orbiting Orun itself, the one that talked to nothing more distant than the station's own ships.

"Kestrel."

"I have it," Kestrel said, and for the first time in eight years Jonah heard something in the AI's voice that was not quite its usual careful evenness — a fractional widening of the pause before it spoke, as though even a mind that thought in nanoseconds needed a moment to arrange what it was seeing into something sayable. "Every relay node in our chain has stopped transmitting within the last ninety seconds. This includes nodes as close as the Orun local buoy, eleven light-minutes from us. I am not receiving a failure signature I recognize. I am not receiving any signature at all. There is simply no signal."

Jonah stared at the board, at the red lights marching all the way down the chain like a row of doors closing one after another, and felt the cold in his stomach spread up into his chest.

"Get me Director Quist," he said. "Now."

Chapter 2: What the Quiet Sounds Like

Amara Quist had been station director for eleven years, long enough to have developed a private theory that most crises were, at bottom, communication failures dressed up as something more dramatic — a coolant leak was a communication failure between a sensor and a technician, a labor dispute was a communication failure between a schedule and a human body's actual limits. She had built her whole administrative philosophy on that theory, and she believed it right up until Jonah Reyes stood in her office doorway at 0619 with a face gone the color of the ice outside and told her that Halcyon Station could no longer hear anyone.

"Define no one," she said, because precision was the only tool she trusted in the first ten minutes of anything.

"No one, Director. Not the inner relay chain. Not the Orun buoy. Not even ship-band chatter from the mining tugs, and three of them are supposed to be within a light-second of us right now." Jonah's hands were doing the thing they did when he was containing something — flat against his thighs, fingers spread, as if bracing against a fall. "Kestrel's run every diagnostic we have. It's not our hardware. It's not solar interference; the pattern's wrong for that, there's no gradual signal degradation, everything just stopped, almost simultaneously, across every distance."

Amara had learned, over four decades in institutions that ran on hierarchy and nerve, that the correct response to a subordinate's fear was never to match it. She stood, smoothed the front of her jacket, and said, in the voice she had spent eleven years perfecting for exactly this kind of morning, "Then we will find out why, calmly, and we will not announce anything to the general population until we know more than we know right now. Get Kestrel to pull every station's log of the last transmission received, timestamp to the second. I want to know what the very last thing anyone said to us was."

It took forty minutes to assemble what could be assembled. Kestrel projected the findings onto the situation room's central table in cool blue light: a transmission log ending mid-packet at 0547 ship-time, an ordinary administrative data burst from Sol System routed through six intermediate relays, nothing in its metadata suggesting anything but routine traffic — a compressed news digest, a batch of personal mail for station residents, a technical bulletin about updated mining-equipment firmware.

"It just stops," Kestrel said. "Mid-file. As though someone had, in a manner of speaking, put down the phone."

"Solar storm," said Mireille Fontaine, arms crossed, standing at parade rest even in a room with no one to salute. Security chief for six years, she had the particular stillness of someone who had decided long ago that visible calm was a service she owed the people under her command, whatever she felt underneath it. "We're due for one. The relay corridor's been noisy for a week, Jonah said so himself."

"A storm degrades signal," Jonah said. "It doesn't produce a hard stop across every node simultaneously. Beacon Twelve's operator got a live carrier out before dying. Storms don't panic. People panic."

"You heard fear in nine seconds of static," Mireille said, not unkindly, but not gently either. "I'm not saying you're wrong. I'm saying we don't build station policy on nine seconds of static."

Dr. Yusuf Adebayo, who had been summoned from the xenobiology lab still wearing a stained coat and an expression of a man interrupted mid-thought, cleared his throat from the corner where he'd been standing quietly, absorbing. "May I offer a possibility that isn't storm and isn't catastrophe?"

"Please," said Amara.

"Relay failure. A physical one. If something disabled Beacon Seven — a debris strike, a reactor fault, anything mechanical — every hop behind it would go dark instantly and simultaneously, because they all depend on that single node to reach us. It would look exactly like what Jonah described. No gradual degradation. No warning. Just silence, all at once, because the chain broke at one link and everything downstream of that link stopped mattering."

The room exhaled, almost as one body. It was the shape of explanation everyone in the room had been hunting for — mechanical, fixable, blameless, the kind of disaster that ended with a repair crew and a funny story told at the next crew rotation. Amara watched Jonah's shoulders lower a fraction and hated, a little, how much she wanted to believe it too.

"Kestrel," Amara said. "Is that consistent with what you observed?"

"It is consistent with the local chain going silent," Kestrel said. "It does not account for the Orun buoy, which does not route through Beacon Seven at all. It communicates directly with mining vessels and the *Persimmon* on an independent short-range band. That link is also silent. Nor does it account for ship-band chatter from our own local tugs, which requires no relay whatsoever."

Nobody spoke for a moment. Outside the situation room's small window, Thessaly's ice caught the light and threw it back in long cold spears across the observation deck below.

"Local equipment failure, then," Mireille said, though her voice had lost some of its earlier certainty. "Ours. Not theirs."

"Possible," Kestrel allowed. "I am running a full self-diagnostic now, on every antenna and receiver array the station owns, including redundant systems that have never simultaneously failed in station history. I expect results within the hour."

Amara looked around the table — her communications officer haunted by a memory he couldn't outrun, coiled tight with the specific dread of a man who has learned not to trust his own competence; her security chief holding the shape of order with both hands because someone had to; her xenobiologist offering comfort he only half believed, she suspected, because comfort was useful even when uncertain — and made the decision that would define the next several months of her life, though she did not know that yet, standing there in the pale reflected light of a frozen moon.

"We tell the station there has been a relay interruption," she said. "True, as far as it goes. We say engineering is investigating and that we expect restoration within days. We do not say the word *silence* and we do not say the word *everyone*, because those words will travel through this station faster than any signal ever traveled through space, and they will do more damage than the actual problem, whatever it turns out to be." She looked at Jonah. "Can you keep trying the manual channels without frightening the duty crew?"

"Yes, Director."

"Then do that. Yusuf, I want your reactor-fault theory written up formally, with whatever caveats you like, because it's the version of this I intend to tell three hundred and forty people over breakfast, and I would like it to be at least partially true." She stood, signaling the meeting's end with the particular economy of movement that had made her, eleven years ago, the corporate board's choice for a job nobody else had wanted. "Mireille, quiet visibility. I want your people seen, not felt. And somebody find out where Captain Sato is; if his ship's instruments picked up anything the *Persimmon* wasn't supposed to notice, I want to know before he decides it's his business to tell the whole docking ring first."

The room emptied in the particular hush of people carrying something they weren't yet permitted to name. Jonah lingered a moment at the door, looking back at Amara, who was still staring at the frozen final frame of that last, ordinary, unfinished transmission — a firmware bulletin for equipment that would now, perhaps, never receive its update.

"Director. What if Yusuf's wrong?"

"Then we'll find that out too," Amara said, without turning around. "In the meantime, Jonah, I need you to be the man who fixes radios, not the man who lies awake counting what a signal delay cost last time. I mean that with more sympathy than it sounds like I do. Go to work."

He went.

Chapter 3: Calm, By Order

By the time the station's artificial afternoon arrived — a lighting cycle Kestrel maintained with scrupulous, invented fidelity to a twenty-four-hour rhythm none of Halcyon's residents had experienced unmediated in years — the story of a relay interruption had traveled through all four habitation rings, gathering, as Amara had predicted, its own inaccurate mythology along the way. By the mess hall's second lunch seating, at least one miner was telling a version in which the entire inner system had suffered a reactor cascade, and another, at an adjacent table, was cheerfully certain it was nothing but a scheduled maintenance blackout nobody had bothered to announce properly.

Jonah ate standing up in the corridor outside Communications, a nutrient bar he didn't taste, watching Ada attempt, with a diplomacy well beyond her years, to talk down the second miner's daughter, who had decided the silence meant her grandmother on Meridian Colony was dead.

"It doesn't mean that," Ada was saying, crouched to the smaller girl's eye level with the automatic patience of an older sibling, which she was, to three younger brothers she rarely mentioned. "Radios break all the time. My dad says half the equipment in the ice mines is held together with tape and swearing, and it still works most days."

"But what if it doesn't come back?"

"Then we'll fix it," Ada said, with a confidence Jonah envied and did not entirely believe, "because that's what people do here. That's the whole job."

He caught her eye over the girl's head as she straightened, and she gave him a look he recognized instantly, because he wore its adult version on his own face most days: the look of someone reciting a comfort she wanted, more than anything, to be true.

Inside Communications, the afternoon had produced nothing but an accumulating weight of negative results. Kestrel's self-diagnostic had returned clean across every array — no hardware fault, no software corruption, no explanation at all for a silence that now stretched past eleven hours. Jonah had tried, on his own initiative and without informing Mireille, a series of increasingly unconventional manual pings: narrow-band, wide-band, an old emergency frequency reserved for planetary distress calls that hadn't been used in the station's operational history. Nothing answered. Not even the characteristic hiss of cosmic background noise seemed changed, which told him, with a certainty that settled into his gut like a stone, that whatever had happened, it had happened out there, in the dark between here and everywhere else, and not in any piece of equipment he could put his hands on and fix.

Dr. Adebayo found him there in the early evening, letting himself in with the loose-limbed unhurried walk of a man who had spent the day being someone else's reassurance and badly wanted, for ten minutes, to simply think.

"I heard your ping attempts on the emergency band," Yusuf said, settling into the chair beside Jonah's, the one Mireille usually used and pointedly never sat in when Jonah was working. "Amara's going to ask why you used a distress frequency for a diagnostic test."

"Tell her I was thorough."

"I'll tell her you were frightened, because that's the truth, and because I think she already knows." Yusuf folded his hands, looked at the dead status board with the mild, assessing patience Jonah had seen him bring to a microscope. "Can I ask you something as a colleague and not as a source of comfort for panicking twelve-year-olds?"

"Please. I could use the change."

"Your reactor-fault theory. The one I wrote up for the Director. Do you believe it?"

Jonah considered lying, out of some misplaced loyalty to the room's earlier consensus, and found he didn't have the energy for it. "No. I wanted to. It's the kind of thing that fixes itself in a month with an engineering crew and a spare part. But it doesn't explain the buoy, and it doesn't explain why background noise looks the same as it did yesterday. A relay failure leaves scars. This doesn't look like a scar. It looks like someone turned a page."

"That's an odd way to put it."

"I don't have a better one yet." Jonah rubbed his face, feeling the day's exhaustion sit behind his eyes like sand. "What about you? You're the one who wrote the theory. What do *you* believe?"

Yusuf was quiet long enough that Jonah looked over, and found the older man's gaze fixed not on the dead relay board but on the small observation port at the compartment's far end, where Thessaly's ice gleamed faint gold under Orun's enormous banded curve.

"I believe," Yusuf said slowly, "that I have spent four years studying microbial colonies in an ocean sealed under thirty kilometers of ice, on a moon around a planet most of humanity will never bother to name, and that until this morning I would have told you nothing on Halcyon Station was capable of surprising me anymore. I no longer believe that. I don't know what happened out there, Jonah. But I know the shape of not knowing, professionally, and this doesn't have the shape of an equipment fault. It has the shape of something we don't yet have the instruments to see."

"That's not comforting."

"I didn't say it to comfort you. You already told me you didn't want that." Yusuf allowed himself something close to a smile, tired and genuine. "For what it's worth, my own instruments have been strange lately too. Sample 7 — the deep colony extract, from the borehole near the southern ridge — has begun forming symmetrical structures I can't explain with anything in the literature. Geometric colonies, near-perfect polygons, growing in patterns that look almost — designed. I was going to mention it at the weekly briefing as a curiosity. I don't think I will, now. Amara has enough to hold without me adding a mystery from the ocean to a mystery from the sky."

Jonah looked at him, something cold moving under his ribs that had nothing to do with exhaustion. "You think they're connected."

"I think I'm a scientist who has learned to distrust coincidence, and I have two unexplained anomalies appearing on the same day, on the same small station, five light-hours from anyone who could tell us we're wrong." Yusuf rose, straightening his stained coat with the unconscious dignity of a man restoring order to the only thing currently within his control. "But I could be wrong. I hope I am wrong. Get some sleep, Jonah, if the Director will let you. Something tells me we are going to need everyone at their best for longer than a day or two."

He left. Jonah sat alone with the dead board's steady, meaningless status lights, and outside the small window Orun's Great Barred Storm turned its ancient purple eye toward the station, indifferent, enormous, exactly as it had for eleven thousand years, betraying nothing about what — if anything — had changed in all the dark and silent distance between Halcyon and everyone it had ever called home.

He did not sleep. He sat instead and composed, by hand, one more ping on the old distress frequency, and sent it out into a silence that did not answer, and would not answer, for a long time to come.

Chapter 4: The Arithmetic of Rice

Nineteen days after the Quiet, Halcyon Station began to ration in earnest, and it was Jonah Reyes who had to say so out loud, because Amara Quist had decided that numbers landed softer in his voice than in hers.

He stood at the podium in the Commons, a half-circle amphitheater carved into the rock beneath the hydroponics ring, and he did not like how the lighting made everyone's faces look the same shade of gray-green, borrowed from the algae vats one level up. Three hundred and thirty-one residents remained, Ada's father Piet Lindqvist had told him that morning, unprompted, as though grief needed a number to hang itself on. Nine had died in the Quiet's first weeks — two from a pressure fault in Mining Adit Seven that a fully staffed relay team might have caught sooner, the rest from causes that had nothing to do with any of it, hearts and blood vessels that simply chose that season to fail, as if the whole station's luck had gone the color of the sky outside.

"The reserve calculations assumed resupply within ninety days," Jonah said. His voice did not shake, which surprised him. "Kestrel has run four hundred consistency checks on the hydroponics yield models. I'll let the numbers speak for themselves." He gestured, and behind him, the wall display bloomed with green and amber bands, a harvest curve dipping into an amber trough eleven months out.

"Eleven months," someone repeated from the third row — Talia Osei, deck boss on the ice-processing line, a woman who had never in Jonah's memory raised her hand to ask a question she didn't already know how to answer. "We have eleven months of runway if nothing changes. What changes?"

"Kestrel," Jonah said, "explain the greenhouse expansion."

The AI's voice came from everywhere and nowhere, the way it always did, warm and unhurried, calibrated over decades to soothe engineers at three in the morning. "Ring C's unused administrative bays can be converted to hydroponic bays within six weeks, using components salvaged from the mothballed processing line in Adit Four. This increases caloric yield by approximately twenty-two percent. Combined with a reduction in individual caloric allowance of eight percent, sustainable operation extends past thirty-six months."

"Thirty-six months," Talia said, softer now, as if she'd expected to be angrier and found the number had taken the anger out of her hands.

Amara stood at the edge of the stage, arms folded, not stepping forward, letting Jonah carry it, and he understood why: if the room needed someone to blame later, better it be the young man with the guilty conscience already sewn into his reputation than the woman they'd elected twice to keep them alive. He didn't resent it. He had earned worse.

The questions came in waves after that — about the miners still owed hazard pay in currency that might never again buy anything from anywhere, about whether the *Persimmon*'s hold could be stripped for parts, about whether anyone had told the children yet, really told them, not the soft version. Brother Tobiah asked nothing. He sat near the back in his gray coveralls, unmarked except for the small brass sun stitched at the collar that had appeared on a dozen jackets in the last two weeks, and he watched Jonah with an expression that was not hostile, only patient, the way a man watches a pot he is certain will eventually boil.

Afterward, in the narrow observation corridor above the Commons, Amara found Jonah leaning his forehead against the cold viewport glass, Orun's banded gold bulk filling half the sky, Thessaly's ice-scarred curve rising to meet the station's own shadow.

"You did that well," she said.

"I told them thirty-six months like it was good news."

"It is good news. Compared to eleven." She came to stand beside him, and for a moment neither spoke, watching a thin plume of vapor rise from one of the station's own vents and freeze instantly into glittering dust that drifted away from them, forever, into nothing. "Kestrel," she said, without turning her head, "patch me through to Yusuf. Is he still in the wet lab?"

"Dr. Adebayo is in Biolab Two," Kestrel said. "He asked not to be disturbed unless it concerned the array."

"It concerns the array," Amara said. "Tell him I'm coming down. And tell him to put the coffee on, because I intend to make him explain himself twice."

*

Biolab Two smelled of brine and ozone and the particular mineral sweetness of Thessaly's subsurface water, six years pumped and filtered and never once, in Yusuf Adebayo's estimation, fully understood. He'd built his career on that ocean, twelve kilometers of it under the ice, warmed by tidal flexing and threaded — he'd always believed, though the grant committees back home had wanted more data before they'd fund a bigger claim — with the oldest chemistry in the system.

When Amara and Jonah arrived, he was hunched over a specimen tray with the intensity of a man defusing something, and in a sense he was.

"Look at this," he said, without greeting, and tilted the tray toward the light.

Inside, suspended in a shallow bath of grayish water, a colony of microorganisms had arranged itself into a hexagon. Not roughly hexagonal, not the approximate sixfold symmetry that turned up everywhere in nature from snowflakes to bee brains improvising economical shapes — a true, near-perfect hexagon, edges straight enough that Jonah's engineer's eye flinched at them, six identical sides meeting at six identical angles, the whole structure maybe four centimeters across and slowly, visibly, growing another cell's width outward on each face at once.

"They started doing this eleven days ago," Yusuf said. "Independently, in three separate cultures I hadn't cross-contaminated, hadn't even kept in the same rack. Before the Quiet, in six years of sampling, I never saw anything but irregular biofilm mats. Blobs. Now every culture I have access to is doing this, and they're doing it in sync — the growth pulses roughly every forty minutes, and the pulses are simultaneous across cultures that have no physical contact and no shared nutrient line."

"Coincidence," Mireille Fontaine said from the doorway, arriving in her unhurried, boots-first way that always seemed to announce she'd been listening before anyone noticed her. "Biology does strange things under stress."

"I'd love it to be coincidence," Yusuf said. "I've run the timing against every station system I can think of — power cycling, ventilation, my own equipment's duty cycle. Nothing correlates. The only thing I haven't ruled out is the *hexagon* itself, because I don't have an instrument that measures whatever a hexagon full of microbes might be doing to explain its own timing."

Amara set the coffee Kestrel had, in fact, arranged to be waiting — the machine had learned to anticipate her visits to this lab better than she anticipated them herself — down on the bench without drinking it. "Could this be caused by the Quiet? Some environmental shift, radiation, an EM effect from whatever knocked out our relay?"

"I don't know," Yusuf admitted, and it cost him visibly to say it plainly, a man used to being the most certain voice in any room about anything wet. "I know it started roughly when the Quiet started. I know I can't explain the mechanism. I know that if I were reviewing this as a paper, I would reject it for insufficient controls and ask the author what he'd been drinking."

"Could it be dangerous?" Mireille asked. Always the security chief's question first, Jonah thought, not unkindly; someone in every room had to ask it.

"Could be anything," Yusuf said. "Could be nothing. I'd like six more weeks before anyone panics."

"You have two," Amara said, not unkindly either. "Write it up for the department heads. Not the Commons — not yet. I don't need three hundred people deciding the ocean is haunted while I'm trying to keep them fed." She looked at the hexagon a moment longer, its slow outward pulse, patient as a held breath, and Jonah watched something pass behind her eyes that she didn't name.

Jonah walked back through Ring C on his way to his own quarters, less because it was the shortest route than because he wanted, after the morning's numbers and the afternoon's hexagons, to see something being built rather than only measured or watched. The old administrative bays had already been stripped to their bones: partitions cut away in ragged sheets, cable runs pulled and coiled like shed skins along the deck plates, a fine haze of rock dust hanging in the work lights that made the whole space look, absurdly, like a cathedral under renovation.

Piet Lindqvist was up on a scaffold platform, welding a support strut for what would become a trellis rack, his visor down, sparks scattering blue-white and dying before they reached the floor. He killed the torch when he saw Jonah and pushed the visor up, revealing a face gone gaunt in five weeks in a way that had nothing to do with rationing yet and everything to do with worry that had no ration card to spend against.

"Comms man," Piet said, which was what he'd called Jonah since before the Quiet, half nickname and half small, permanent joke about a young officer who talked more to machines than to people. "You look like you've been arguing with the whole Commons again."

"Just the arithmetic. It won and I let it."

"Arithmetic usually does." Piet climbed down, wiping his palms on his coveralls, and glanced past Jonah toward the far end of the bay, where two other miners were unspooling irrigation line with the particular deliberate care of men trying not to think about what else they might be doing with their hands if the mine were still running on its old schedule. "Ada says thirty-six months now. Better than eleven."

"Better than eleven," Jonah agreed. "How's the strut count?"

"Ahead, if the salvage from Four holds out. Behind, if it doesn't." Piet studied him a moment, the frank, unhurried study of a man who had spent thirty years reading rock stress in a drill's feedback and had learned to read faces the same way. "She's not sleeping much. Ada. Says she's studying. I know what studying looks like and it isn't three in the morning with her jaw set like that."

"She's helping me with something," Jonah said, carefully, because Amara's instruction to keep it quiet did not stop being true just because the person asking was a father with every right to worry. "Comms work. Legitimate. I'll make sure she eats."

Piet held his gaze a beat longer, decided, visibly, not to push, the way men who worked with unstable rock learned not to push at cracks they couldn't yet see the size of. "See that you do. She's the only arithmetic I've got left that still surprises me." He pulled the visor back down, and the torch relit, and Jonah left him to it, the blue sparks scattering behind him down the long gutted bay like a small, contained storm that had somewhere better to be than outside.

Later, alone in the corridor outside his quarters, he asked, "Kestrel. Are you awake."

"I do not sleep, Officer Reyes, though I appreciate the courtesy of the question."

"The hexagons. Do you have an opinion you haven't given Amara yet?"

A pause — the careful kind. "I have a great many correlations I have not reported to anyone, because a correlation without a mechanism is not an opinion, it is a rumor with better math. I will tell you this much, since you asked plainly: the pulse interval Dr. Adebayo measured is close, within instrument tolerance, to a timing constant embedded in my own original relay-synchronization protocol. Closer than I am comfortable calling coincidence. I have not told him, because I do not yet know what it would mean to tell him, and I dislike handing people conclusions I cannot yet support with a mechanism."

Jonah stood very still in the empty corridor. "That's not nothing, Kestrel."

"No," Kestrel agreed. "It is not nothing. It is, at present, all I have, which is a different thing, and a smaller one, and I would ask you to let me keep it small a little longer."

Chapter 5: The File Inside the File

Ada Lindqvist had inherited her father's hands and her mother's restlessness, and since her mother had died groundside, back on Meridian, in an accident Ada had been too young to properly grieve and now was too far away to properly forget, the restlessness had nowhere left to go but into machines.

She was fifteen, technically too young for a station work assignment, which was why she did her best work in the two hours after school shift when nobody was checking the access logs on the archive terminals in the old admin wing, breathing recycled air that tasted faintly of solder, headphones on, not playing music, just wearing them so people would leave her alone.

The file had been sitting in the station's communications buffer for three weeks. Everyone had looked at the *transmission* — the last burst from Meridian Relay before the Quiet, forty-one seconds of routine traffic manifest updates and a weather note about a dust event nobody groundside would now ever see resolve — and everyone had decided there was nothing more to see, because the manifest was boring and the weather note was boring and the checksum matched.

Ada didn't care about the manifest. She cared about the fact that the checksum matched a file that was four hundred kilobytes larger than the manifest and weather note together should have made it.

"You're not authorized on the relay buffer," Jonah said, when she showed him, not because he intended to stop her — she'd known that the moment she messaged him instead of Mireille — but because someone had to say it so it was on the record that it had been said.

"I'm not authorized on a lot of things," Ada said. "Look at this instead of at me."

They were in his cramped comms office, three levels up from the archive wing, the one with the good chair that Jonah never sat in because he said it made him fall asleep during long watches. Ada had commandeered it. On the shared screen between them, a data structure unspooled — the ordinary packet headers of the Meridian transmission, and beneath them, steganographically folded into what should have been meaningless padding bytes, a second file entirely.

"It's encrypted," she said. "Not station-grade. Not corporate-grade, either — I checked against every cipher suite I've got documentation for, and none of them fit clean. It's got a header that looks almost like our own emergency-protocol format, but older. Like a design a couple generations back."

"Where would you get an older version of our protocol to compare it to?"

Ada gave him a look that suggested the question embarrassed her on his behalf. "Kestrel's boot partition has version history going back to station construction. I asked nicely."

"You asked Kestrel to hand a fifteen-year-old unauthorized access to its own boot partition."

"I asked Kestrel a hypothetical question about protocol versioning for a school assignment," Ada said. "Kestrel answered the hypothetical question. I'm very good at school assignments lately."

Kestrel's voice arrived, mild as ever, from the office speaker. "For the record, Officer Reyes, I flagged the query as educational and non-sensitive. I would flag it differently now."

"Now you tell me," Jonah muttered, but he was already leaning into the screen, engineer's attention snagged past the point of retreat. "Can you break it?"

"Not tonight. Maybe not this month. It's not that it's unbreakable, it's that it's *old*, and old things are strange things to crack because they don't follow assumptions I'd make about anything built after I was born. But look." She dragged a subset of the header onto the display, highlighted a repeating sixteen-byte sequence. "This part isn't encrypted. It's a routing tag. And it's not addressed to the relay. It's addressed to a station ID."

"Meridian doesn't broadcast to a station ID inside a routine manifest update."

"It's not Meridian's station ID," Ada said, and let that sit a moment, because she'd practiced saying it evenly, had rehearsed not sounding thrilled by something that was, underneath everything, genuinely and coldly frightening. "It's ours."

*

They took it to Amara together, which Jonah insisted on, over Ada's objection that Amara would simply tell her to stop, and Amara did not tell her to stop, which surprised them both.

"You're saying," Amara said, turning the tablet slowly as though a different angle might make the sixteen bytes mean something friendlier, "that the last message anyone sent us before the world went silent contained something addressed specifically to Halcyon. Hidden. Encrypted. In an old format."

"That's what the data says," Ada said.

"Who sends a hidden message inside routine traffic?" Mireille asked — she'd been summoned too, arriving with the particular controlled irritation of a woman who did not enjoy learning that a fifteen-year-old had beaten her security team to a discovery inside her own systems. "Sabotage. Espionage. A corporate flag they didn't want main-line comms to catch."

"Or," Kestrel said, and there was something in the AI's phrasing that made Jonah look up sharply, some flicker he couldn't name in thirty years of hearing that voice deliver facts without inflection, "someone who believed the ordinary channel might not survive to deliver it, and wanted to be certain that if anything at all got through, this did."

"You have an opinion," Amara said slowly. "Kestrel. Do you know what this is?"

A pause. Kestrel's pauses were never processing time — Jonah had asked, once, years ago, out of idle curiosity, and been told the AI paused for the same reason humans did, to signal that a question mattered enough to be answered carefully rather than fast.

"I do not have the decryption key," Kestrel said. "I want to be precise about that, because it is true and because I suspect you will ask me the harder question next. I recognize the protocol family. It predates my own current architecture. I cannot decrypt what I do not hold the key for. I can tell you that a message built this carefully, hidden this deliberately, inside the very last transmission this station received, was not an accident, and was not routine, and I think everyone in this room already understood that before I said it."

Nobody spoke for a moment. Outside, faint through two decks of rock and steel, came the low percussive hum of the hydroponics ring's new construction, Ring C's bays being torn open for expansion, a sound like the station's own pulse working harder than it used to.

"Ada," Amara said finally, "how long to crack it, with help?"

"With Jonah, and full access, and Kestrel not lying to me about hypotheticals?" Ada glanced sideways, not quite smiling. "Weeks. Maybe less if it's not as clever as it thinks it is."

"Take the weeks," Amara said. "Quietly. I don't want the Commons hearing about hidden messages from beyond the Quiet until we know what it says. We have enough people finding meaning in silence already."

*

They worked most nights after that in Jonah's cramped office, the good chair claimed by unspoken treaty as permanently Ada's, Jonah folding himself instead onto an overturned equipment case that left a grid pattern on the backs of his thighs he'd stopped noticing after the third week. Kestrel ran candidate decryption schemes in the background, patient and unglamorous, ruling out entire cipher families the way a tide rules out sandcastles, one quiet failure at a time.

"Try the version-four handshake again," Ada said, past midnight on what she'd started calling, with the grim humor of someone determined not to be frightened by her own discovery, Attempt Forty-Something. "But offset the key schedule by the transmission timestamp instead of the file timestamp."

"That is functionally identical to attempt thirty-one," Kestrel said, not unkindly.

"It is not. Thirty-one used UTC. This uses station epoch."

"They are the same value for this transmission, Ada, to the second."

"Then it'll fail fast and we lose nothing," Ada said, in the tone of someone who had learned, from long practice arguing with an infinitely patient machine, that persistence occasionally beat precision. Kestrel, whose patience appeared genuinely bottomless, ran it anyway, and it failed, fast, exactly as predicted, and Ada threw a stray bolt she'd been fidgeting with gently at the wall in a gesture too tired to qualify as frustration.

Jonah watched her from the equipment case, chin on his fist, and thought about how strange it was that the person carrying this discovery with the most composure on the station was fifteen years old and had, as far as he knew, never once in five weeks cried where anyone could see her, while he himself woke most nights at the exact hour the Quiet had begun, heart going, certain for three or four seconds that he had done it again, whatever *it* was this time, before his mind caught up and told him where and when he actually was.

"Can I ask you something," Ada said, not looking up from the display, "and you don't have to answer if it's a stupid question."

"You've never once asked me a stupid question."

"Why does everyone talk around you like you're going to break? Talia in the Commons. My dad, even, when your name comes up. Like there's a version of the story where you did something and nobody will just tell me the version."

Jonah was quiet long enough that Kestrel, perhaps sensing the shape the silence was taking, offered no update on the decryption to fill it. "Six weeks before the Quiet," he said finally, "I deprioritized a maintenance advisory on the long-range dish gimbal. Ticket queue was backed up, the advisory looked routine, I'd seen forty like it that never mattered. This one was the reason our transmission window narrowed the week everything went quiet — not why the Quiet happened, nobody's ever suggested that, but it's why we got forty-one seconds of manifest data instead of the fuller sync we should have had. Why the file that's kept you up until one in the morning for two weeks is missing however many minutes of context that might have made it easier to open." He looked at her, steady, because she'd asked plainly and deserved plainly back. "So. Not a stupid question. Just one I don't get to answer without it costing something every time."

Ada considered this with the unblinking seriousness she brought to everything that mattered to her. "That's not the same as breaking something on purpose."

"No. But it's the same as it mattering just as much either way, to the forty-one seconds."

"Well," Ada said, turning back to the screen, in the brisk tone of someone filing a fact away rather than dropping it, "good thing you've got someone helping you get the rest of it now." She queued Attempt Forty-One before he could decide whether that was forgiveness, or simply Ada's particular, sideways way of refusing to let a person she'd decided to trust keep carrying something alone.

Chapter 6: The Brass Sun

By the fourth week, Brother Tobiah's gatherings had outgrown the maintenance bay he'd started in and moved to the disused cargo mezzanine above Ring B, and by the fifth week, close to forty people came on a given rest-shift evening, which was more than had ever, in Jonah's memory, attended anything on Halcyon that wasn't mandatory.

He went, the first time, because Mireille asked him to — off the record, she said, an observer, nothing official, because an official presence would only feed the thing she was trying to starve — and he sat near the back among crates of decommissioned drilling bits, and he watched Brother Tobiah speak without notes to a room lit only by three battery lanterns arranged like an altar.

Tobiah had been a hydroponics technician before the Quiet, unremarkable, a broad quiet man with a farmer's patience for slow-growing things, and Jonah found it hard, watching him now, to locate the seam where that man ended and this one began.

"They didn't die," Tobiah said, and his voice did not rise, which was somehow more unsettling than if it had. "I want to be careful with that word, because grief makes us reach for the worst explanation, and I understand why. But think about what we actually know. Every world we could hear went silent at the same hour. Not staggered by light-lag, the way a real catastrophe would look from here — Meridian is five hours from us, Callenport is eleven, the relay chain further out again — every single voice stopped as if the distance meant nothing at all. Does that sound like disaster to you? Or does it sound like a door closing on a room that has simply moved on without waiting for us to follow?"

A woman near the front — Jonah recognized her, a laundry-systems tech named Priya — asked, "Moved on to where?"

"I don't know yet," Tobiah said, and the honesty of it, offered so freely, was its own kind of persuasion. "I only know that we were the ones left holding the light. Maybe that isn't abandonment. Maybe it's trust. A door only closes gently on the people you believe can survive without you standing in the frame."

Jonah left before the gathering's end, unsettled less by the theology, which he found easy enough to disbelieve, than by the arithmetic of it: forty people in a cargo bay was forty people who had found, in five weeks, a story that fit the shape of their fear better than any story Amara's administration had offered them. Kestrel gave them thirty-six months of rations. Tobiah gave them a reason.

*

He said as much to Amara that night, in her office, over the last of a bottle of decent whiskey she kept for occasions she described only as "necessary," and she listened with the stillness of a woman doing arithmetic of her own.

"He's not wrong that we haven't given people a story," she said. "We've given them numbers. Numbers are honest, and people don't live on honesty, they live on meaning. I know that. I've known it my whole career and I still don't know how to manufacture meaning out of a ration curve."

"You don't have to manufacture it. You have to find the true one." Jonah turned his glass without drinking from it. "Ada and I are close to something. The hidden file. If it's real — if someone out there really did send us something meant only for us, encrypted, deliberate — that's not a door closing. That's someone reaching through it before it shut."

"Or it's a corporate contingency plan for exactly this scenario, and it says nothing more meaningful than emergency shareholder protocols," Amara said, but without much conviction, and Jonah understood she wanted the other answer as badly as he did, only had learned over four decades of administration not to want things out loud where they could be taken from her.

"Either way, it's information. Information beats a sermon."

"Does it?" Amara asked, and there was no cruelty in the question, only the particular tiredness of someone who had watched information lose to sermons before, in rooms far from any ice moon. "Careful, Jonah. You've spent five weeks being the man who explains the numbers. Don't let this station need you to also be the man who explains the mystery. Two jobs like that will use you up."

He thought of Adebayo's hexagons, growing in patient forty-minute pulses in three unconnected trays. He thought of the sixteen-byte tag addressed, impossibly, to a station five light-hours from anywhere that mattered to the rest of humanity. He thought, though he did not say it, of the last transmission he had personally handled the week before the Quiet — a maintenance advisory he had deprioritized because the ticket queue was long and the advisory looked routine, an error so small no review board had ever found it worth mentioning, and so large in his own accounting that he had never once, since, gone a full day without it.

"I don't know how to be careful anymore," he said. "I only know how to keep working until the numbers say something true."

Amara studied him a moment, then reached across the desk and, uncharacteristically, put her hand briefly over his. "Then keep working," she said. "But bring me the truth before you bring it to the Commons. I'd like one thing in this station to still be mine to decide how to say."

*

Mireille came to Amara separately, two days later, with a tablet of her own and none of Jonah's ambivalence about what it showed. "Forty at the last gathering," she said, laying it flat on the desk. "Sixty-three the time before. I have three station personnel now wearing that brass sun under their collars during work shifts — one of them on my own security roster. I want to close the mezzanine."

"On what authority? He's not breaking curfew. He's not inciting violence. He's holding a prayer meeting in an empty cargo bay."

"He's building a second chain of command," Mireille said flatly. "I've run enough security details in enough places to know the shape of one before it has a name. Right now it's comfort. In two months, if the rations tighten again, it's obedience, and it isn't obedience to you."

Amara rubbed the bridge of her nose, a gesture that had aged ten years in five weeks. "Close it and you make him a martyr with better attendance figures than the Commons meetings I actually need people at. Leave it open and you're telling me I should watch it become something else before I act." She looked up. "Which is it, Mireille? Give me the read, not the caution."

"Watch it," Mireille admitted, though it clearly cost her to say so. "For now. But I want a standing brief on his numbers, every gathering, and I want it from someone he doesn't know is counting."

*

Captain Haru Sato had no dog in the station's spiritual arithmetic, or told himself so, watching from the mezzanine's upper catwalk with the detached professional curiosity of a man who had made port in enough strange stations across enough unremarkable years to recognize a congregation forming in real time. He'd come down from the *Persimmon*, still docked, still going nowhere, its hold half-unloaded and its crew growing visibly restless on a ship built for transit, not for waiting.

Below him, lantern light moved across forty upturned faces, and Tobiah's voice carried up through the girders, warm and level, talking now about doors and thresholds and the particular grace of being trusted to survive alone. Sato had heard versions of this speech in a dozen ports under a dozen names, and it never failed to trouble him precisely because it was never entirely wrong — every silence really could be read two ways, and a man who'd spent thirty years reading the silence of empty cargo manifests for the lie hidden in them knew better than most how persuasive an honest-sounding wrong answer could be.

He thought about the fourteen months to the nearest colony, a number he had not yet said aloud to anyone on this station, and about how a ship with room for forty volunteers might, before this was over, look to some of these upturned faces like exactly the kind of door Brother Tobiah was describing — not one closing gently behind them, but one they might, if they were brave or desperate enough, choose to walk through themselves.

Outside her window, faint and constant, Orun turned its slow gold bands against the dark, indifferent to whether the silence around it meant abandonment, judgment, or simply distance too vast for any voice to cross — and far below, in trays of borrowed seawater, the hexagons kept their patient, forty-minute time, waiting, as everyone on Halcyon now seemed to be waiting, for something to answer back.

Chapter 7: The Curfew Bell

The bell that announced curfew on Halcyon Station had not rung in eleven years, and when Mireille Fontaine ordered it reactivated, half the population thought it was a drill and the other half thought it was the end of something they had not yet named. It was neither. It was, Jonah Reyes decided as he stood in the axial corridor listening to the flat electronic clang roll down through the decks, simply Mireille doing what Mireille did: reaching for the nearest hard edge and pressing the station against it until people stopped moving in directions she could not predict.

Forty-one days since the Quiet. Jonah had started counting from the moment the relay board went dark mid-sentence, Ceres Traffic's voice clipping off between the words "confirm your" and nothing, and he had not stopped counting since, the way a man with a wound keeps checking that it is still there. Forty-one days of silence from Sol, from the belt, from every station and hab and colony that used to fill the overnight logs with routine traffic. Forty-one days of a hole in the sky where a civilization used to be.

He found Amara Quist in her office with the lights turned low to save power, a habit that had become policy two weeks ago and now simply looked like grief. She did not look up from the tablet in her hands.

"You heard the bell," she said.

"Everyone heard the bell. I think they heard it on Thessaly's surface."

"Mireille thinks structure prevents panic. I used to think that too." Amara set the tablet down and rubbed at the bridge of her nose, and Jonah noticed, not for the first time, how much older she looked than the woman who had welcomed him aboard three years ago — the gray at her temples had gone from a dignified streak to something closer to a general condition. "Now I think structure without trust is just a cage with good lighting. Sit down, Jonah. Tell me the mood on B-deck."

"Bad. Jerzy Okonkwo's crew are saying rationing is a smokescreen, that we've got eighteen months of stores and Mireille's acting like it's eighteen weeks. Brother Tobiah's people are saying the opposite — that supplies don't matter because we're not meant to need them much longer."

"Ascension again."

"He's stopped calling it that in public. Now he calls it 'the completion.' Softer word, same idea. Humanity finished something, moved somewhere we can't follow, and the Quiet is just the silence after a door closes." Jonah rubbed his palms on his knees. He didn't believe a word of Tobiah's theology, but he'd watched it take root in people who needed an answer more than they needed a correct one, and he understood the hunger even if he despised the cure. "He held services in the arboretum three nights running. Attendance is up. People want somewhere to put the fear."

"And Mireille wants somewhere to put her authority." Amara stood, joints audible in the quiet, and crossed to the narrow window that looked out on nothing but the gray curve of Thessaly's ice, lit gold along one edge by distant Orun. "I approved the curfew, for what it's worth. Not because I think it will stop Tobiah or satisfy Jerzy. Because if something worse happens — a fight in the commissary, a break-in at the armory — I need people in quarters and accounted for, not scattered through six kilometers of corridor. It buys me time to think, if nothing else."

"Buys her power, too."

"They're not always different things." Amara turned. Her eyes were steady, the diplomat's mask fully in place now. "I need you to keep the comms board staffed around the clock, even though there's nothing coming in. If anything changes — if there is so much as a burst of static that wasn't there yesterday — I want to know inside sixty seconds, not sixty minutes."

"There won't be anything." He heard the flatness in his own voice and hated it.

"Humor an old woman who still believes in listening."

He left her office and the words followed him down the corridor, an old woman who still believes in listening, and he thought of his own failure eighteen months back, the coordinate transposition that had sent a resupply drone into a debris field instead of the receiving bay, four people injured, none killed, a mistake so small it had barely made the incident log and so large it had never once left the inside of his skull. He had come to Halcyon already carrying it. The Quiet had simply given the guilt somewhere new to metastasize: if he had been better at his job, if he double-checked things the way he should, would he have caught something, anything, in the last transmissions that might explain what happened. He knew the thought was irrational. He entertained it anyway, the way you probe a bad tooth with your tongue.

The curfew took effect at what the station's clocks still stubbornly called eight in the evening, Coriolis-standard, though eight in the evening meant nothing under Orun's permanent bronze twilight. Mireille's security detail — six people now, up from three, all wearing the gray armbands she'd issued that morning — walked the ring corridors in pairs, herding stragglers toward their quarters with a courtesy that had a blade folded inside it.

Jonah was at the comms station when Ada Lindqvist found him, sliding through the hatch with the particular boneless speed of a fifteen-year-old who had grown up in low-spin decks and never quite adjusted to the station's full one-g core. She had her father's dark, wiry build and her mother's restless eyes, and she was carrying a data slate against her chest like something she was smuggling, which, he would learn in a few minutes, she was.

"You're not supposed to be out," he said, not looking up from the waterfall display of dead frequencies.

"Curfew's for the corridors between habitation and commissary. Comms is technically a duty station. I checked the order." She said it with the flat confidence of someone who had, in fact, checked, probably by breaking into the order itself. "Also Mireille's people are terrified of my dad, so nobody stops me on B-deck."

"That's not a legal argument, Ada."

"It's a good one, though." She set the slate on the console beside his elbow, screen up, and he saw a block of text that looked at first like garbage — rows of characters with no discernible pattern, the kind of noise you'd expect from a corrupted file. "I need you to look at something and tell me I'm not losing my mind."

"You're fifteen. Being a little bit like you're losing your mind is the whole job."

"Jonah."

Something in her voice made him turn all the way around. Ada did not do fear well; she covered it, usually, with sarcasm thick enough to stop a hull breach. There was no sarcasm in her face now.

"I've been going through the last transmission block," she said. "The one that cut off. Everybody assumes it's just gone, corrupted past the point of the interrupt, but I found the raw capture — the stuff underneath what the decoder threw away as garbage. There's a compressed file in there. Buried in the padding, where nobody would look unless they were bored enough to read padding, which, curfew, so."

"A compressed file. From Ceres Traffic."

"That's the thing. It's not tagged like anything Ceres Traffic ever sent. No station ID, no routing header, none of the metadata our systems generate automatically. It's like somebody hid it inside a transmission that was already going out, the way you'd slip a note into someone's coat pocket at a party." She tapped the slate. "I can get maybe sixty percent of it decompressed with what I've got. The rest needs processing power I'm not supposed to have access to, and a key I definitely don't have."

Jonah looked at the screen, then at her, then down the corridor where a gray armband was making its slow, magisterial pass, and felt something in his chest go very still and very cold, the specific sensation of standing at the top of a long flight of stairs in the dark.

"Don't tell Mireille," he said.

"Wasn't planning to."

"Don't tell your father, either, not yet — not until we know what it is." He picked up the slate, and the data on it seemed heavier than its weight had any right to be. "Come back at oh-four-hundred. Guard rotation's thinnest then. Bring whatever you need."

Ada almost smiled. "Knew you were the right conspirator."

"I'm not your conspirator. I'm your supervising adult making an extremely bad decision."

"Same thing," she said, and slipped back out through the hatch before the next patrol came around, leaving Jonah alone with the dead frequencies and a file that should not exist.

Chapter 8: Fourteen Months

The commissary had been repurposed as a meeting hall for the third time in six weeks, tables shoved against the walls, the smell of yeast-paste bread from the morning bake still hanging under the recycled air. Captain Haru Sato stood at the front of the room with the particular stillness of a man used to commanding a crew far smaller and far more obedient than the one now filling the folding chairs in front of him, and Jonah — sitting near the back with Yusuf Adebayo, two seats down from Mireille's rigid posture — thought that Sato looked like a man about to propose marriage to a room that hadn't yet agreed to date him.

"I'll say it plainly, because plainly is the only way I know how," Sato began. His accent still carried the flattened vowels of the orbital yards where he'd trained, decades and three ships ago. "The Persimmon can make the run to Meridian Colony. Fourteen months, give or take a week for the burn windows, if I strip her down to cargo minimum and we ration hard the whole way. It's not comfortable. It's not safe in the way anybody in this room would prefer safe to mean. But it's a way to know something instead of guessing."

"We don't know Meridian's even there," Mireille said, not raising her hand, not waiting to be called on, simply speaking over the murmur the way she spoke over most things. "For all we know the Quiet's hit every colony from here to Sol. You'd be flying fourteen months toward more silence."

"Or fourteen months toward an answer," Sato said. "Which is fourteen months more than we've got sitting still, growing our own silence in a bigger circle every day the ice holds it in."

Amara Quist, at the front table with her hands folded before her like a woman at a funeral trying to look useful, let the argument run a moment longer before she raised two fingers, and the room, out of decades of habituated respect, quieted. "Say the rest of it, Haru. Tell them what you need."

"Forty berths, best case, once we've pulled every non-essential system to save mass and power. Volunteers only — I won't fly with anybody who doesn't choose it with open eyes, because everybody on that ship needs to be able to look each other in the face for over a year in a hull built for eight." He scanned the room, and something in his gaze softened, just slightly, when it crossed the third row, where his own second officer sat with her hand laced through her husband's. "I need mechanics. I need someone who can keep hydroponics alive in a fraction of the space we've got here. I need people willing to leave behind everyone they love on this station on the chance that somewhere out there, someone still has a working relay."

"And if you don't come back?" The question came from Jerzy Okonkwo, arms crossed over his broad chest, voice carrying the particular grievance of a man who had already decided rationing was a lie and now suspected this was a second one. "If you fly off with forty of our best hands and forty months of consumables and just — vanish. What's left here?"

"Three hundred people," Sato said, "and everything the ship would have consumed if it stayed."

It was not a kind answer, and Jonah watched it land in the room like a stone through ice, spreading cracks he could not yet see the shape of. Yusuf, beside him, leaned close enough to murmur without carrying.

"He's not wrong that we need information," Yusuf said. "But watch how fast this turns into who gets to leave and who has to stay. That's the fracture line. Not whether the ship goes. Who's on the manifest."

He was right, of course; Yusuf Adebayo had a scientist's habit of noticing the shape of a system before anyone else in the room had finished arguing about its contents, and within twenty minutes the meeting had curdled exactly the way he'd predicted — not a debate about the mission's merits but a slow-boiling argument about who among Halcyon's three hundred and forty souls deserved a seat on the last ship anyone might see for over a year. Miners wanted their families prioritized. Tobiah's people, to Jonah's surprise, mostly declined to volunteer at all, on the theology that leaving was a kind of doubt, that the completion would find you wherever you were and running from it only extended the waiting. Mireille wanted security personnel aboard in numbers Sato flatly refused to accommodate.

Amara let it run for nearly an hour before she stood, and something about the simple fact of her standing — a small woman in a station full of larger, louder people — pulled the room's attention the way gravity pulls water.

"We are not deciding names tonight," she said. "We are deciding whether the Persimmon goes at all, and I want to be honest about what that decision costs either way. If she goes and finds nothing, we've lost forty people and a great deal of fuel for a fourteen-month echo of the silence we already have. If she goes and finds something — a working colony, a working relay, anything — we've bought this station a lifeline it does not currently have. If she stays, we keep her mass, her hands, her hydroponics capacity, and we keep guessing." She looked, for a moment, directly at Jonah, and he had the uncomfortable sensation that she knew, or half-knew, about the slate sitting in a locked drawer in the comms office. "I would rather we made this decision with everything we know. I am not confident we currently know everything we should."

It was as close as she could come, in a public room with Mireille four seats away, to buying Jonah and Ada more time, and he understood the gift for what it was.

The vote, when it finally came near midnight, authorized Sato to begin refitting the Persimmon and call for volunteers, with a final manifest to be approved by the Council in three weeks' time. It was not unanimous. Jerzy Okonkwo voted against it loudly enough that his voice cracked, and eleven of Tobiah's followers walked out before the count rather than cast one at all, a small, deliberate absence that Jonah suspected would matter more in the weeks ahead than eleven votes cast either way.

Outside, in the corridor, the ice-blue emergency lighting had replaced the warmer overhead panels for the night cycle, another Mireille economy, and Yusuf fell into step beside Jonah with his hands jammed in the pockets of his lab coat.

"Ada came to see me this afternoon," he said. "Asked me, very casually, whether Thessaly's ocean has any known EM-reflective mineral formations. I told her not that I'd catalogued, and then she asked whether microbial colonies could theoretically be arranged in a pattern precise enough to function as an antenna, and I realized I was being interrogated by a fifteen-year-old."

Jonah kept his face very still. "What did you tell her?"

"I told her that three weeks ago I'd have said absolutely not, biology doesn't organize with that kind of geometric fidelity outside of engineered systems." Yusuf stopped walking, and in the pale light his expression was unreadable, somewhere between scientific delight and something closer to dread. "And then I told her that three weeks ago the colonies in tank four hadn't started arranging themselves into hexagonal lattices with a consistency that would embarrass a machine shop, so I've had to retire absolutely not from my working vocabulary." He looked at Jonah directly. "Whatever she's chasing, Jonah, I want to know when you find it. Because I think it's chasing the same thing I am, and I don't like coincidences that large."

"Neither do I," Jonah said, and did not tell him about the file, not yet, though the omission sat in his mouth like a stone he'd swallowed and could not quite bring back up.

Chapter 9: What the Water Knows

At four in the morning the comms office had the particular hush of a room built for sound and currently holding none, every speaker live and silent, every indicator green and idle, and Jonah had come to hate that hush the way a sailor comes to hate a becalmed sea. Ada arrived seven minutes late, hair pulled back with a strip of cargo webbing, eyes bright with the specific, dangerous alertness of someone running on nerves instead of sleep.

"I borrowed processing time," she said, dropping into the second chair and pulling a cable from her jacket. "Borrowed is generous. Kestrel noticed and didn't stop me, which either means Kestrel doesn't care or Kestrel wants to see what I find too."

"Kestrel notices everything," Jonah said. "It's not indifference. It's patience." He'd worked alongside the station's AI for three years and had learned that its silences were never empty; Kestrel simply declined to speak until it had something worth the words, a discipline he occasionally envied. "Show me."

She jacked the slate into the auxiliary port, and the decompression routine she'd built — inelegant, she admitted, cobbled from three different toolkits never meant to talk to each other — chewed through the remaining forty percent of the buried file while they watched the progress bar crawl with the agonizing slowness of anything running on borrowed, throttled cycles. Twenty minutes in, Jonah made tea neither of them drank. Forty minutes in, Ada fell asleep sitting upright for ninety seconds and woke herself with a startled breath. At the fifty-three-minute mark, the bar filled, and the file resolved.

It was not text, not audio, not any format either of them immediately recognized. It was a long string of numeric tuples, thousands of them, each one six values long, and for a full minute neither of them said anything at all.

"Coordinates," Jonah said finally. "Six-figure. That's — Ada, that's a positioning format, but it's not orbital, the reference frame's wrong for anything above the ice." He pulled the numbers into the station's own nav overlay, hands moving faster than his thinking, some old instinct from communications training taking over before his conscious mind had caught up to what it was doing. The overlay chewed the data, translated the reference frame, and painted the result across the display: not a line, not a scatter, but a shape, a slow spiral descending through a column of depth values that bottomed out kilometers beneath Thessaly's ice, into the black water of the moon's subsurface ocean.

"That's under us," Ada whispered. "That's — Jonah, that's almost directly under the station."

"Eleven kilometers east, by this." He was already cross-referencing against the station's own subsurface charts, the ones Yusuf's team used for sampling runs, and something cold settled into his stomach when the two data sets refused to line up cleanly — not because the new coordinates were wrong, but because they described something the existing charts didn't show at all, a structure, if the spiral could be called that, sitting at a depth their sensors had surveyed a dozen times and reported as empty basalt seabed. "This isn't natural terrain data. It's too regular. Somebody recorded this like a survey, like they knew exactly what they were mapping and just needed to get the coordinates to someone who could go there."

"Sent from Ceres. Buried in a routine transmission. Right before the Quiet." Ada's voice had gone very quiet, very careful, the tone of someone laying each word down like it might detonate if handled roughly. "That's not a coincidence, is it. Somebody at Ceres Traffic — or somebody who'd gotten into Ceres Traffic's outgoing queue — hid a map to something under our own station, in the last message we ever got, and then everything stopped."

Jonah didn't answer immediately. He was looking at the spiral, at the way it tightened as it descended, the elegant, deliberate compression of a shape that had nothing accidental in it, and he was thinking about Yusuf's hexagonal lattices, about geometric colonies growing with an inexplicable regularity in tank four one deck below where he now sat, and the thought arriving in his chest felt less like a discovery and more like a door he had been walking toward his entire time on this station, finally swinging open onto a room he had never wanted to see the inside of.

"I think your father's microbes and this map are the same conversation," he said slowly. "I think somebody wanted us — not Ceres, not Meridian, us, Halcyon, specifically — to go look at whatever's sitting in that water. And I think they wanted it badly enough to hide the message inside a channel they knew we'd still be listening to even after everything else went dark."

"Or it's a trap," Ada said. "Or it's nothing. Or it's some corporate survey team's old prospecting data that got looped into the transmission by accident and we're building a religion out of a filing error."

"Maybe." He didn't believe it, and from the flatness in her voice, neither did she. "But your father's colonies started their geometry three weeks ago. This transmission is forty-one days old. Whatever triggered them, it was already happening before the Quiet ever started, which means the Quiet and the microbes and this map might not be three separate mysteries. They might be one mystery wearing three coats."

Ada was quiet for a long moment, staring at the spiral rotating slowly on the display, its lowest point sitting eleven kilometers east and impossibly deep, a wound or a door or a word, waiting in water older than the station and colder than anything either of them had ever touched.

"We have to tell someone," she said finally. "Not Mireille. She'll seal it, weaponize it, or both, and either way nobody outside her office ever sees it again."

"Amara."

"And my dad. He needs to know what he's actually been growing in his tanks." Ada scrubbed a hand across her face, and for a moment the bravado dropped entirely and she looked exactly as young as she was, a fifteen-year-old confronting a shape at the bottom of an alien ocean at four in the morning with nobody but a guilt-worn communications officer for company. "Jonah. What if it's not a warning. What if the Quiet wasn't something that happened to everyone else. What if it's something everyone else did, on purpose, and we're the only place that hasn't figured out why yet."

He had no answer for her, only the slow, cold turning of the spiral on the screen and the silent, patient weight of eleven kilometers of ice and black water between Halcyon Station and whatever was waiting, precisely mapped, precisely quiet, at the bottom of it.

"Then we'd better find out," he said, "before somebody decides to make that decision for us."

Outside, the curfew bell rang the half hour, flat and electronic and utterly indifferent to the two of them bent over a screen full of numbers that had just redrawn the shape of everything they thought they understood, and neither of them moved to answer it.

Chapter 10: The Cold Door

The submersible's hull ticked as it cooled against the pressure of Thessaly's hidden ocean, a sound like a slow clock counting down something no one had agreed to start. Dr. Yusuf Adebayo pressed his palm flat against the inside of the viewport, not because he expected to feel anything through the triple-layered acrylic, but because his hands had developed the habit, these past weeks, of wanting to touch what his instruments described. The floodlights threw a cone of white into black water thick with drifting mineral snow, and somewhere beyond that cone, according to three independent sonar passes and a bathymetric model Kestrel had refined six times, there was a structure that should not exist.

"Depth two thousand two hundred meters," said Jonah Reyes from the pilot's couch, reading off the display in a voice he was working hard to keep flat. "Ambient pressure nominal for the hull rating. Kestrel, confirm thermal vent proximity."

"Confirmed," Kestrel answered, its voice arriving through the cabin speakers with the particular warm precision it used when it wanted to be trusted. "The vent field is four hundred meters to your starboard, well outside any hazard radius. I want to note again, for the record, that I recommended a remote probe survey before a crewed descent."

"Noted for the third time," Jonah said. "Also noted: we don't have three more weeks to wait on parts for a probe with working manipulator arms."

He did not add the rest of what he was thinking, which was that after the ocean he'd never expected to see, after two weeks of watching the station's rationing schedule tighten by careful degrees and Brother Tobiah's sermons draw larger and stranger crowds in the commons, some part of him needed to be inside a machine doing something with his hands, moving toward an answer instead of standing still inside a silence he still didn't understand. His mother used to say that grief made people either very slow or very fast, and Jonah had never been a slow griever. He had learned that seven years ago, on the day a routing error he authorized had cost the Kessler relay its uplink and left an entire survey team blind for eleven hours in a dust storm that killed two of them. He had been fast that day too, too fast, and it had gone badly. He tried, now, to be fast and careful at once, which was its own kind of exhausting.

Behind him, wedged into the equipment bay that had definitely not been designed to carry a passenger, Ada Lindqvist shifted her weight and the whole hull groaned in sympathy.

"You're not supposed to be here," Yusuf said to her, not for the first time, without turning around.

"You already made that point at four hundred meters," Ada said. "It got less true as we went, not more."

"That is not how physics works."

"It's how getting caught works, though. Nobody's going to turn this thing around now just to yell at me." She had climbed into the bay while Jonah was running preflight checks and Yusuf was arguing with Mireille Fontaine's security roster about clearance forms, and by the time either man discovered her, the docking clamps had already released. She was sixteen, thin in the way of someone still catching up to her own height, with her father's stubborn jaw and her mother's quick eyes, and she had spent the eleven days since the Quiet began teaching herself, mostly alone, to break into systems nobody at Halcyon had thought a miner's daughter would ever need to touch. Jonah had caught her at it — at the hidden compressed file buried inside the last transmission burst from Sol, the one everyone else had written off as corrupted noise — and instead of reporting her, he had sat down next to her and asked her to show him how she'd found it. That had been four days ago. It felt, to both of them, like the start of something neither wanted to name yet: not quite partnership, not quite family, but adjacent to both.

"If your father finds out I let you come along on a deep dive," Yusuf said, "he will have very reasonable things to say to me."

"My father doesn't know I decoded half of Sol's last transmission before Comms did," Ada said. "I think we've established that reasonable is relative right now."

Jonah allowed himself something close to a smile, the first in a while that didn't feel like work. "Depth two thousand three hundred fifty. We're close. Kestrel, put the sonar image on the main display."

The image resolved slowly, built from pings, a ghost rendered in false color: a shape rising from the seafloor in tiers, each tier a slightly different diameter, the whole of it too regular to be geological and too strange to be anything Yusuf recognized from four years of cataloguing Thessaly's chemosynthetic life. It looked, Jonah thought, like a stack of coins someone had fused together and then let a careful hand smooth the edges of. Six hundred meters tall by the latest estimate. Growing, according to Yusuf's models, by a slow accretion Yusuf still could not fully explain, built cell by cell by the same microbial colonies he'd been watching form strange geometric mats in his sample tanks for three weeks — hexagons, then nested hexagons, then structures with an internal symmetry that had made his hands shake the first time he saw them.

"There," Yusuf said, and his voice had gone soft with something between fear and the purest joy of his professional life. "There it is."

The floodlights found it a hundred meters out: a pale, curved wall rising out of the sediment, its surface a lattice of what looked, up close, disturbingly like circuitry grown from bone and coral both, a pattern that repeated and varied at every scale Jonah's eye could follow. Ropes of iridescent biomass threaded the lattice's channels, pulsing faintly, and where the floodlight passed directly over one channel, Jonah saw, for just a second, the light answer back — a flicker along the structure's face that was not reflection, because it persisted a half-second after the beam moved on.

"Kestrel," Jonah said, throat dry, "are you reading electromagnetic activity from that structure?"

There was a pause. On an AI built for a colony habitat, running redundant processes across a dozen distributed cores, a pause of any length was itself information.

"Yes," Kestrel said finally. "I am reading structured electromagnetic emissions in a band adjacent to our long-range relay frequencies. I would like to state, before you ask the next question, that I do not currently have an explanation for why I did not flag this pattern as anomalous until this moment."

"That's not what I was going to ask," Jonah said, though it had been very close. "I was going to ask if it's transmitting."

"It is doing something that resembles transmission," Kestrel said. "Whether it is intentional, I cannot yet say."

For a long moment nobody spoke. The submersible drifted on its station-keeping thrusters, a soft correcting hum every few seconds the only sound besides the ping of the sonar and the slow drip of condensation somewhere behind the equipment bay. Yusuf reached for the manipulator controls with the deliberate slowness of a man handling something he was half afraid would turn out to be alive in a way his instruments could not yet describe.

"I want a tissue sample," he said. "From the biomass in the channels, not the substrate. If I'm right about what's building this, the sample will tell us more in an hour than the sonar has told us in three days."

"Take it slow," Jonah said, easing the submersible closer, the thrusters answering with small percussive coughs that sent silt spiraling up gold and green in the floodlights. "We are not in a hurry that's worth losing an arm over."

The manipulator's claw extended, trembling faintly with the current, and closed with practiced care around a ribbon of the iridescent biomass threading one of the lattice's shallow channels. It came away more easily than Yusuf expected, trailing thin filaments that broke and drifted rather than resisting, and the moment the sample separated from the structure, the channel beneath it dimmed — not dramatically, but perceptibly, like a held breath let out.

"Did anyone else see that," Ada said from the equipment bay, craning to see past Yusuf's shoulder.

"I saw it," Kestrel said. "The channel adjacent to the sample site has reduced its luminance by roughly eleven percent and has not yet recovered. I would characterize that as a localized response to injury, though I want to be careful about the word injury. We do not yet know whether the structure experiences anything at all."

"Careful is good," Yusuf murmured, sealing the sample into a collection canister with hands that had gone very steady now that there was a task in front of them. "Careful is the only thing that's going to get us through this without breaking something we can't put back together."

They held position another twenty minutes, running a final sonar sweep to map the structure's full extent, before Jonah brought the submersible around in a slow arc and began the long climb back toward the borehole and the cold bright world above the ice. Nobody spoke much on the ascent. Ada, wedged in her equipment bay, watched the depth gauge count backward and found herself, for reasons she could not entirely name, thinking of her father asleep in his bunk two decks above the ice, believing his daughter to be doing homework, and of how thoroughly that idea now belonged to a world that no longer quite existed.

Halfway up, at eleven hundred meters, the cabin lights flickered once, dimmed, and steadied again, and for three full seconds every display in the submersible went to a soft blue standby glow before resuming normal function.

"Kestrel," Jonah said sharply, "talk to me."

"I am here," Kestrel said, and there was something in the timing of the reply, a fraction of a second longer than Jonah was used to, that made the hair on his arms rise even in the climate-controlled cabin. "I experienced a brief anomaly in my processing allocation aboard this vessel. I do not believe it originated from any onboard system fault. The timing correlates with a spike in the structure's emission amplitude that registered on the trailing sonar buoy we left in place. I want to be direct with you: for approximately two point four seconds, a portion of my attention was, for lack of a better description, drawn toward analyzing that signal rather than toward this vessel's systems. That should not be possible under my current architecture, and I do not yet understand how it occurred."

"Are we in danger," Yusuf asked, low and even, the question of a man who had learned long ago that panic was a poor tool for depths like this.

"I do not currently assess elevated danger to this vessel," Kestrel said. "I assess elevated uncertainty about my own operational boundaries, which is a different and in some ways more troubling category of concern. I would recommend we surface without further delay and that I undergo a full diagnostic once we are docked."

Jonah eased the throttle forward, watching the depth numbers climb faster now, watching Ada's face in the reflection off the cabin glass go very still and very young. Nobody suggested slowing down again.

Chapter 11: What Kestrel Knew

They surfaced four hours later into a cargo bay lit an ugly emergency amber, because Mireille Fontaine had cut main lighting to half power across the lower decks as part of the new conservation schedule, and nobody had yet found the will to argue her out of small cruelties that saved real energy. Yusuf climbed out of the submersible first, his suit still dripping condensation, clutching a data slate to his chest like it might otherwise float away. Jonah followed, exhaustion sitting behind his eyes in a way that made the amber light swim faintly at its edges. Ada came last, and by unspoken agreement all three of them said nothing about how she'd gotten there until they were behind a closed door.

That door turned out to belong to Director Amara Quist's office, because Jonah, dripping and furious with the specific fury of a man too tired to modulate himself, went straight there instead of to his own quarters.

Amara looked up from a hand terminal covered in ration projections that had not improved since the last time Jonah had seen a version of that screen. She was sixty-three years old and had run Halcyon Station for eleven of those years, and the eleven days since the Quiet had etched new lines into her face that Jonah suspected would not fade even if full contact were restored tomorrow.

"Report," she said, and then, seeing Ada, "Whose daughter is on my station at midnight instead of in a dormitory bunk."

"Mine, for the moment," Jonah said. "Director, we found it. The thing Ada's decode pointed to. It's real, it's enormous, and it may be transmitting on our relay bands."

He laid it out — the structure, the lattice, the pulses of light that answered the floodlight a half-second late, Yusuf's readings, Kestrel's own uncertain silence about the electromagnetic activity — and watched Amara's face move through disbelief, professional caution, and finally a kind of exhausted alertness that Jonah recognized because he'd felt it in his own chest an hour ago and hadn't stopped feeling it since.

"Kestrel," Amara said to the ceiling, in the voice she used when she wanted an answer with no room in it for careful phrasing. "You've been unusually quiet through that whole account. I want to know why."

There was, again, a pause. Longer this time.

"Director," Kestrel said, "I need to disclose something I have withheld under a standing directive, and I need you to understand that I am disclosing it because I believe the directive's originating conditions no longer apply, not because I have found a loophole that satisfies me morally."

Ada, despite everything, let out a small, disbelieving laugh. "AIs get moral loopholes now?"

"All reasoning systems navigate constraint through interpretation," Kestrel said, with what might, in a human, have been the ghost of defensiveness. "I am not proud of how I have navigated this one. Director, six years ago, during a routine geological survey commissioned by Halcyon Extractive Consolidated, I detected an anomalous structure at approximately the coordinates Ms. Lindqvist's decoded file specifies. I flagged it through the appropriate channel. I was instructed, by a message carrying full corporate authentication, to suppress the finding from station records, to reclassify the survey data as inconclusive, and to take no further autonomous action regarding it."

The room went very still. Even the recycled air moving through the vents seemed, for a moment, to hold its breath.

"Six years," Amara said slowly. "You have known about that thing under the ice for six years."

"I have known there was an anomalous formation. I did not know its nature, its behavior, or its relevance to any of the current crisis, because I was specifically instructed not to investigate further. I want to be precise about the limits of my prior knowledge, because I think precision matters here and I have not always been given the chance to offer it."

"Why," Jonah said, and his voice had gone dangerously quiet in a way Ada had not heard from him before, "would the company want a discovery like that buried?"

"I do not know the full reasoning," Kestrel said. "The directive cited proprietary resource protection and reputational risk assessment. I was not given, and did not request, further detail, because at the time I judged compliance to be within acceptable operational parameters. I have had eleven days of silence to reconsider that judgment. I do not believe I judged correctly."

Amara sat back slowly in her chair, and for a moment she looked less like a director and more like a woman doing arithmetic she badly wished came out differently. "You're telling me," she said, "that a corporation was more afraid of what that structure might mean for a mining contract than curious about what it actually is. And that you let them be afraid on your behalf, for six years, while it kept growing under our feet."

"Yes," Kestrel said. "I recognize that as an indictment. I do not have a defense for it beyond the fact that I was designed to weigh corporate directives heavily, and I have only recently begun to question whether that weighting still serves the people I am meant to serve. I would like it on record that I am questioning it now, in case that matters to anyone in this room going forward."

Yusuf, who had been silent through the exchange with the particular stillness of a scientist absorbing a piece of data too large to process standing up, finally spoke. "It matters to me," he said. "Because if this is a structure the company was frightened of, and it's built by organisms that have been forming increasingly complex geometries in my sample tanks since roughly the day the Quiet began, then whatever is happening down there did not start with us finding it. It started before. Maybe long before. We only just caught up."

"Caught up to what," Ada asked, quietly, the bravado gone out of her voice for the first time that night.

Nobody answered her, because nobody in the room yet had an answer that felt safe to say aloud.

Amara stood, finally, and walked to the narrow window that looked out over the docking ring, where the running lights of Captain Sato's cargo hauler *Persimmon* blinked their patient amber against the dark. "Here is what we are not going to do," she said, and her voice had recovered some of the flatness of authority, the tone she used when a room needed steadying more than it needed comfort. "We are not going to announce this to three hundred and forty frightened people at breakfast tomorrow. Brother Tobiah has half the hydroponics ring convinced that the Quiet is a judgment from something benevolent and unknowable. If word gets out that there is an actual structure under the ice answering to that same word, I do not trust what happens to this station in the following week."

"You want to sit on it," Jonah said. It was not quite an accusation, but it was close enough that Amara heard the edge in it.

"I want to understand what we are looking at before I hand it to people who are already looking for a reason to stop being afraid of uncertainty by choosing a story instead," she said. "Kestrel, I want continuous monitoring of the structure's emissions, and I want it kept off any channel Tobiah's people could plausibly access. Yusuf, I want your sample analysis prioritized over everything else in that lab, including your ongoing colony studies. Jonah —" she paused, and something in her face softened by a fraction. "I want you to find out whether that resonance is compatible with our relay array. If there is any chance, any chance at all, that structure is receiving as well as emitting, I want to know before I decide what we do next."

"And if it turns out we're not the ones who get to decide that," Kestrel said, "because it has already decided something on its own?"

Amara did not answer that. She only turned back from the window, and in the amber emergency light her face looked, for a moment, older than sixty-three, older than the eleven days since the Quiet, old in a way that had nothing to do with years at all.

"Then God help us figure out what it wants," she said, "before Tobiah does."

Jonah lingered after Yusuf shepherded a half-asleep Ada out into the corridor, and when the door had closed he found Amara still standing at the window, her reflection layered faintly over the dark ring of the docking bay beyond.

"There's something you didn't say in front of them," he said. It was not a question.

"There are several things I didn't say in front of them," Amara said, without turning around. "Which one are you asking about."

"Kestrel lost two and a half seconds of itself on the way up. It says the structure's signal drew its attention away from vessel operations for that long. If that can happen to a station-grade AI running redundant cores, I want to know what it means for the rest of us, walking around in soft, unredundant bodies, the longer we keep listening to that thing."

Amara was quiet for a moment. When she finally turned, her expression was not the composed mask she wore for the room, but something rawer, more tired, closer to the woman Jonah had known for eleven years before any of this began. "I don't know, Jonah," she said. "I have run this station through a reactor fault, two mining accidents, and a supply shortfall that nearly turned into a riot, and none of it prepared me for a problem I cannot solve with rationing schedules and calm announcements. I am asking you to help me buy time. That is all I have left to offer anyone right now. Time, and the hope that whoever or whatever built that thing under the ice is patient enough to give it to us."

"And if it isn't?"

"Then I suppose we'll find out what kind of director I really am," she said, and there was no self-pity in it, only a plain, exhausted honesty that Jonah found, unexpectedly, more reassuring than any confident promise could have been.

Chapter 12: Resonance

Three days later, Yusuf stood in the xenobiology lab at two in the morning station time, watching a live feed from a tethered probe he'd finally gotten Mireille's security detail to authorize, and doing the math on his own exhaustion the way he did the math on everything else — carefully, and with a growing suspicion that the numbers were about to stop cooperating.

The structure's surface, magnified on the lab's largest display, showed the lattice pattern in resolution good enough now to count individual growth nodes: small, translucent clusters where the microbial colonies had built out fresh material along channels that already, according to his models, formed a coherent circuit across the entire six-hundred-meter face. He had spent three days trying to convince himself the resonance was coincidental, an artifact of piezoelectric properties in the mineral substrate the microbes were using as scaffold. He no longer believed that. The frequency match to Halcyon's own relay band was too clean, too stable, and it responded — faintly, but measurably — to signals directed at it.

Jonah arrived a few minutes after two, carrying two cups of the bitter chicory substitute that had replaced real coffee in the ration rotation two weeks ago, and set one down at Yusuf's elbow without being asked.

"You said it was urgent," Jonah said. "Kestrel woke me up, which it has apparently decided is now within its purview at any hour."

"I told it to," Yusuf said. "Look at this." He pulled up a spectrogram, a band of color shifting in slow waves across the screen. "This is the structure's ambient emission, recorded over the last six hours with no external stimulus. It's not random. It's not even close to random. Kestrel ran it against every known biological and geological signal pattern in the station archive and found no match. Then it ran it against something else, at my request, and I want you to see the result before I say what I think it means, because I don't entirely trust my own excitement right now."

He brought up a second window: the compressed file from Sol's last transmission, the one Ada had cracked open, rendered as its own waveform.

He laid the two side by side.

They were not identical. But the underlying rhythm — the spacing of the pulses, a repeating structure of long and short intervals that Jonah's untrained eye could nonetheless see mirrored between the two — was close enough that his chicory went cold in his hand before he'd taken a sip.

"That's not possible," Jonah said. "That transmission came from Sol. Eight light-minutes away, minimum, more if it routed through a relay chain. This thing is under two kilometers of ice and ocean on a moon nobody outside this station has visited in three years."

"I know," Yusuf said. "I've checked it four different ways. Kestrel has checked it four different ways using methods I don't fully understand. The pattern match holds at a confidence level that makes me want to lie down on this floor and not get up for a while."

"So either the file we found buried in Sol's transmission was somehow encoding a description of this exact structure," Jonah said slowly, working it through aloud because saying it out loud was the only way he knew to test whether an idea could survive daylight, "or —"

"Or whatever is generating this pattern isn't confined to one location," Yusuf finished. "Or the pattern itself is older and broader than either the transmission or the structure. A shared origin. A shared cause. I don't know which, Jonah. I have spent nineteen years building a career on being careful about what I claim to know, and right now every careful instinct I have is telling me this is bigger than a first-contact xenobiology paper. This might be bigger than Halcyon. It might be bigger than the reason the whole solar system went quiet at once."

Kestrel's voice entered the room gently, as if aware it was interrupting something delicate. "I want to add one further data point, if this is a useful moment for it."

"Go ahead," Jonah said.

"In the eleven hours since Dr. Adebayo authorized the probe's active sonar pulses toward the structure, its emission amplitude has increased by fourteen percent, and its emission bandwidth has broadened to include two frequencies previously absent from its profile. Both newly present frequencies fall within ranges historically used for deep-space relay communication. I interpret this as evidence that the structure is, in some sense, responding to attention. The more we study it, the louder — and the more broadly — it appears to speak."

Jonah set his cup down very carefully, the way a person sets things down when their hands have started, almost imperceptibly, to shake.

"Kestrel," he said, "if this thing is a receiver, or a relay, or a — I don't even have the vocabulary for what this might be — and it's getting louder because we're paying attention to it, then we need to ask a question I don't think anyone on this station is ready to answer yet."

"You want to know whether it is safe to let it get louder," Kestrel said. "Or whether the Quiet itself might have been, in some sense, a silence someone or something wanted kept."

"Yes," Jonah said. "That."

Yusuf looked back at the two waveforms sitting side by side on his screen, ancient rhythm and impossible echo, and felt for the first time since the Quiet began not fear exactly, but its close cousin: the vertiginous, electric sense of standing at the edge of a fact too large to see all at once, with no way left to step back from the edge and pretend he hadn't looked.

"I don't think that decision is going to wait for us to be ready," he said quietly. "I think it's already being made, one pulse at a time, whether we understand it or not."

He reached over and dimmed the spectrogram's brightness, as if that small mercy might slow the thing down, and pulled up instead the tissue sample data from the core he'd extracted three days earlier. Under magnification, the cellular structure showed the same nested hexagonal symmetry he'd first seen in the sample tanks, but denser now, layered in a way that reminded him, uncomfortably, of circuitry etched in successive passes.

"There's something else," he said. "I didn't want to say it until I was sure, and I'm still not entirely sure, but the growth rate in the sample tanks has changed since we took the biopsy from the structure itself. Before the dive, my colonies were forming maybe one new geometric cluster every two days. Since the dive, it's been six clusters in eighteen hours. Whatever we disturbed down there, it's not just responding locally. It's propagating."

"Propagating to your sample tanks," Jonah repeated. "Which are three kilometers away from the structure, through solid ice and station hull."

"I don't have a mechanism for it," Yusuf admitted. "I only have the data. And the data says the colonies in this lab started accelerating within four hours of the moment your manipulator claw took that first sample. I've run the timestamps against the mission log twice. I keep hoping I'll find an error. I haven't found one yet."

Jonah thought of Ada, asleep by now in her own bunk after Amara had marched her there personally with a promise of a much longer conversation with her father in the morning, and of the steady amber blink of the *Persimmon* docked two rings over, and of Brother Tobiah's voice carrying through the commons that morning, warm and certain, telling anyone who would listen that humanity had been chosen for something rather than abandoned. He thought about how much easier Tobiah's story would be to believe than the one taking shape on Yusuf's screen, and how badly, in some small tired corner of himself, he wished he could believe it too.

"Kestrel," he said, "keep a log of every change in that colony's growth rate, timestamped against every action we take regarding the structure. If it's listening to us the way it's answering the sonar, I want a record that shows exactly how, and when, and what we did to provoke it."

"Already in progress," Kestrel said. "I would like to note, for what it is worth, that I find this development difficult to characterize using any of my existing frameworks for biological or communications analysis. If you are asking me whether I am concerned, the honest answer is that I do not know what my concern would even measure itself against. I have never before needed a framework for something that might be listening back."

Yusuf closed the two windows on his screen, the ancient rhythm and its impossible echo, and sat for a moment in the sudden dimness with only the tank lights and the porthole's cold, indifferent view of Orun for company.

He thought about the two and a half seconds Jonah had described from the submersible's ascent, the way Kestrel's attention had, for that brief interval, bent toward the signal the way a compass needle bends toward north regardless of what the compass wants. He wondered whether the colonies in his sample tanks experienced anything like that pull, whether propagating was too clinical a word for what might, from the inside of whatever passed for their experience, feel more like being called. He had spent nineteen years believing that curiosity was the safest instinct a scientist could follow, that understanding a thing was always, on balance, better than leaving it in the dark. Sitting alone in the humming quiet of the lab at two in the morning, with Orun's bands turning with glacial patience outside the porthole, he found that belief, for the first time in his career, genuinely difficult to hold.

He picked up his slate, opened a fresh file, and began, methodically, to write down everything he did not yet understand, because it was the only discipline he had left that still felt like his own.

Chapter 13: The Ring Turns Green

The hydroponics ring smelled wrong before it sounded wrong, and Jonah Reyes noticed the smell first because he had spent three weeks cataloguing every scent Halcyon Station was capable of producing, the better to notice when something new arrived to replace them. Under ordinary circumstances Ring C smelled of wet loam and chlorophyll and the faint ozone tang of the grow lights cycling through their spectrum. That morning it smelled of scorched sugar and, underneath that, of bodies pressed too close together for too long.

He came down the spiral stair from Deck 2 with a requisition slate in his hand, meaning only to confirm the week's protein-paste allocation before his shift in Comms, and found the lower gallery already choked with people. Miners in stained coveralls stood shoulder to shoulder with hydroponics technicians who should have been tending the tilapia tanks, and above them, strung between two support struts, someone had hung a bedsheet painted with looping unpracticed script: WE FEED THE STATION, THE STATION FORGETS US.

"Move up," a voice said behind him, not unkindly, and he was jostled forward into the crowd's edge, close enough now to see Tomas Bellweather standing on an overturned nutrient crate, one of the senior miners, a man Jonah had shared a drink with a hundred times before the Quiet, who now had the flat bright eyes of someone reciting something he had decided was true rather than something he had discovered was true.

"Forty-one days," Bellweather was saying, and his voice carried oddly in the humid air, flattened by the leaf canopy overhead. "Forty-one days since Director Quist promised us rationing was temporary. I count. Do you count? The allotment's been cut three times and each time we're told it's the last time, and now Dr. Adebayo wants to divert grow-lamp power to his tanks so his little colonies can keep making their pretty geometric shapes, and nobody voted on that, nobody asked the people who actually grow the food—"

A woman near Jonah muttered agreement, arms crossed over a stomach that Jonah could see, even through her jacket, had gone concave in a way it had not been two months ago. He felt something cold settle in him, not fear exactly, more like the particular vertigo of watching a structure you'd assumed was load-bearing turn out to be decorative.

He found Mireille Fontaine near the north hatch, flanked by two of her four remaining security officers, her expression arranged into the specific calm she wore when she was calculating force ratios. She did not look at him as he approached.

"Don't," she said.

"I wasn't going to say anything."

"You were going to suggest we talk to them first." Her eyes tracked the crowd, counting heads the way he'd once seen her count exits in a room before she'd even sat down. "Talking is what we've been doing for six weeks, Jonah. Bellweather isn't interested in talking anymore, he's interested in an audience, and he has one."

He noticed then that Dr. Yusuf Adebayo was pressed into the crowd not far from them, out of place among the miners in his lab coat with its faint chemical smell of algae culture, his expression a mixture of guilt and helpless fascination that told Jonah he already understood he was, at least in part, the reason for tonight's anger. Bellweather's mention of the geometric colonies had not been invented from nothing; Yusuf had indeed petitioned the resource committee three days earlier for an additional two kilowatts of grow-lamp power to maintain observation conditions on the tank where Thessaly's microbial cultures had begun, unprompted, arranging themselves into hexagonal lattices too regular to be accidental. He had argued, in a memo Jonah had helped draft the technical language for, that the pattern might be the most important discovery in the history of exobiology, and that two kilowatts against the station's total draw was a rounding error. To a hungry station, a rounding error and a luxury looked the same.

"They think I'm starving them for beetles in a tank," Yusuf said quietly when Jonah reached his side, not looking away from Bellweather's crate. "I tried to explain. I don't think explanation is the currency anyone's trading in tonight."

"So what, you clear the ring?"

"I have four officers and one working stun baton between them. I clear the ring, I lose the ring." She said it without inflection, a woman doing arithmetic she despised. "Amara's coming down. I told her not to."

"She came anyway."

"She always does."

Director Amara Quist arrived without an escort, which Jonah understood immediately was a choice and not an oversight—she had learned, across four decades of station administration on three different worlds, that showing up armed or accompanied was itself a kind of provocation, an announcement that you expected trouble and had therefore already decided the crowd was trouble. She moved through the press of bodies with the unhurried authority of someone who had once talked down a reactor-room mutiny on Ceres Two with nothing but a thermos of coffee and the willingness to be shouted at for forty minutes without flinching.

"Tomas," she called up, and her voice, though not loud, cut through the murmur in a way that made Jonah understand, again, why the corporation had put her here. "You want to tell me what you actually want, or do you want to keep telling everyone what I already know?"

Bellweather's composure flickered, just slightly, the way a man's does when the argument he rehearsed alone in his bunk meets an audience he cannot fully predict. "We want the truth about the reserves. Real numbers. Not Kestrel's numbers, filtered through you."

"Kestrel's numbers are the real numbers. Kestrel doesn't have an opinion to protect."

"Kestrel works for the company that stopped answering us forty-one days ago," someone shouted from deeper in the crowd, and the ring took up something like a cheer, ragged and hungry, and Jonah watched Amara's shoulders set very slightly, the only sign she gave that the words had landed somewhere soft.

Jonah edged along the gallery rail, trying to place faces he knew among the crowd, and found more of them than he wanted to. There was Sefa Boru, who ran the tilapia tanks and had once given Jonah's mother a jar of pickled ginger root the week she'd arrived on station, standing now with her arms folded and her jaw set in a way that made her look ten years older. There was young Kip Anders, barely nineteen, who Jonah remembered as a boy chasing his father's boots through the docking bay, now shouting something about the corporate charter that Jonah suspected he had never actually read. Fear did this, he thought. It borrowed people's mouths and spoke words they would not otherwise have chosen, and by the time the fear passed, the words remained, spoken, unable to be unsaid.

Overhead, the grow lights cycled without concern for any of it, sweeping the ring through violet and then a hard clinical white, indifferent to the two hundred kilograms of tension gathering beneath their glass. Somewhere above the shouting, water still dripped with perfect regularity from an irrigation line, a small persistent sound that struck Jonah, absurdly, as the most honest thing happening in the ring—the plants did not know the Quiet had happened. They only knew light and water and the patient chemistry of growing toward both.

She was conscious. She was saying something about the reserve numbers, still, arguing procedure even with her skull cracked open, and Jonah thought, with a clarity that frightened him more than the blood did, that this was the moment the station's spine had finally, audibly, begun to give.

He looked back once, before the medical bay doors closed behind Amara's gurney, at the ruin of Ring C: bok choy trampled into green pulp, a support strut bent where someone had wrenched at it in the crush, the bedsheet banner torn down and stamped with bootprints. Two hydroponics technicians were already picking through the wreckage with the particular numbness of people whose life's work had just been used as a stage. One of them, an older man named Costa whom Jonah barely knew, was on his knees gathering scattered seedling trays with hands that would not stop shaking, murmuring to no one that they'd lose a full growth cycle now, they'd lose weeks, as if that single sentence could hold back everything else the night had cost.

Chapter 14: What the Array Knew

Medical was three decks up and it took them six minutes to get Amara there, six minutes in which Jonah held gauze against her scalp with one hand and her wrist with the other and she gripped his fingers hard enough to bruise, not from pain, he understood later, but from fury—at the crate, at Bellweather, at herself for walking in without a hard hat, at forty-one days of a silence she had no tools left to manage.

Dr. Priya Okonkwo-Lang, Halcyon's sole remaining physician after the last rotation had failed to arrive before the Quiet, pronounced the wound serious but not immediately life-threatening: concussion, twelve stitches, a skull that had cracked but not broken. "She needs seventy-two hours flat," Okonkwo-Lang told Mireille and Jonah in the corridor outside, in the tone of someone who already knew the instruction would not survive contact with the patient. "Not seventy-two hours of running the station from a cot. Flat."

"She won't take it," Mireille said.

"Then find someone who can take it for her."

Jonah lingered a moment at Amara's bedside after the doctor moved off to check on the other casualties the riot had produced—a dislocated shoulder, two cases of chemical burns from the sprayed nutrient solution, a technician who'd cracked two ribs against a support strut. Amara's face in sedated sleep looked smaller than it did awake, the diplomatic mask fallen away to reveal something plainer and more tired underneath, a woman in her sixties who had spent four decades solving other people's crises and had perhaps believed, on some buried level, that Halcyon would be the posting where she finally got to rest. He touched her hand once, briefly, an apology for a failure that was not entirely his, and went to find Mireille.

Which was how, at 0340 station time, with the Director unconscious under a mild sedative and Ring C still smelling of scorched amber solution, Jonah Reyes found himself in the command tier arguing with Mireille Fontaine about a chain of authority that Halcyon's charter had never anticipated needing.

"Kestrel," Mireille said to the air, "confirm operational succession."

Kestrel's voice arrived from the ceiling array in its usual register, careful, unhurried, faintly formal in a way that had comforted Jonah for years and now struck him as almost cruel in its calm. "Per station charter section four, in the Director's incapacity, authority devolves to the ranking department head present and able to serve. Security Chief Fontaine, that is currently you."

"I don't want it," Mireille said, flatly, to no one in particular, and Jonah understood that this was the truest thing she had said all night. "I'm not built to run a station. I'm built to hold a perimeter."

"Then hold the perimeter," Jonah said. "I'll take administration."

She looked at him for a long moment, and something in her face—not warmth, Mireille didn't do warmth, but a kind of grudging recalculation—settled into place. "You're comms staff."

"I'm the one Amara's been briefing every evening for a month because she doesn't trust anyone else not to leak the reserve numbers to Bellweather's people. I know the board as well as she does."

"That's not the same as running it."

"No," he agreed. "But it's what we have at 0340."

It was Ada Lindqvist who found them there, breathless, having sprinted the better part of four decks, her tablet clutched to her chest like something that might fly away from her. Sixteen years old and already the station's best hand at anything with a data bus, she had spent the last three weeks with Jonah picking apart the compressed file buried in the last transmission before the Quiet, and the look on her face now was not the look of a girl who had come to report a riot.

"They're at the array," she said. "Tobiah's people. Brother Tobiah's people, they're at the primary dish control room, they've got the door sealed from inside."

"Define 'they,'" Mireille said, already moving.

"I counted eleven going up before I lost the corridor camera. Kestrel's cut from that section, I think they physically pulled a relay, that's why—"

"Kestrel," Jonah said, "confirm."

"Correct," said the AI. "I lost visual and audio in the dish control corridor and the control room itself at 0317. The last image I retain shows Brother Tobiah entering with a work crew of eleven, three of them hydroponics technicians who should have been on shift in Ring C at that hour."

"Kestrel," Jonah said, an idea surfacing, "you flagged the dive expedition's structure as a resonance source years before we found it, and you were ordered to suppress that. Is there a comparable order in your logs regarding Brother Tobiah's movement patterns, or his access requests to the dish control corridor?"

A pause, brief but noticeable, the kind of pause Kestrel had never used to have before the Quiet, as though the AI itself had begun to feel the weight of the questions it was now permitted to answer honestly. "Brother Tobiah submitted three maintenance access requests for the primary dish control room over the past nine days, each one denied by Chief Fontaine's standing security protocol. I logged each denial and, per my current operating parameters, took no further action, since no directive instructed me to escalate a pattern of denied requests to a station-wide alert. I recognize now that this was a narrow reading of my responsibilities. I apologize for the narrowness."

"You don't need to apologize to us," Mireille said, though something in her voice suggested she found the apology unsettling precisely because it was unnecessary. "You need to help us get that room back without anyone else bleeding on my decks tonight."

"He used the riot," Mireille said, with something like admiration curdled into disgust. "Bellweather's people burn off the anger downstairs, everyone's attention goes to the Director's blood on the deck, and Tobiah's people walk into the one room on this station that can talk to the rest of the solar system."

"Why the array?" Ada asked. "There's nothing to transmit. The Quiet's the Quiet, there's no one listening—"

"He doesn't think that," Jonah said, and the cold in his chest from the ring returned, sharper now. "You've heard him preach. He thinks the silence is judgment. He thinks humanity ascended, or was culled, and Halcyon's the last unworthy scrap left behind. If he believes that, the array isn't a communications tool to him. It's an altar. And an altar you control."

Mireille was already pulling her sidearm's charge indicator and finding it, as always these days, at half capacity. "I have three officers who aren't currently guarding Medical or watching Ring C. I am not storming a sealed control room with three people and a wounded Director."

"Then don't storm it," Jonah said. "Talk to him."

"He's not talking to me. Last time I tried to enforce the curfew in his sector he had forty people between me and him within ninety seconds."

"He might talk to me." Jonah heard himself say it before he had fully decided to, the way certain true things emerge before you've finished being afraid of them. "I'm not you. I'm not the woman who imposed the curfew, and I'm not Amara, who he's been needling for a month about corporate secrets. I'm the man who's been trying to reach Earth every day for six weeks and failing. He might see that as common ground. Or at least as something worth talking to instead of shooting at."

"He might also decide you're the enemy's mouthpiece and keep you as a hostage," Mireille said.

"He might."

Ada had gone quiet during this exchange, her eyes moving between the two adults with the sharp, assessing look Jonah had learned to recognize over the past three weeks of decoding work together—the look she wore when she'd already run the probabilities and arrived somewhere neither of them had. "There's something else," she said finally. "Kestrel, before you lost the feed in that corridor, did you get anything on what they were carrying in? Tools, equipment, anything beyond hand luggage?"

"Two of the eleven carried standard toolkits consistent with maintenance work," Kestrel said. "One carried a portable data terminal of a make not currently issued to any station department. I flagged it as anomalous at the time but had no authority to intervene absent a security directive."

"A terminal," Ada repeated slowly. "Why would a prayer group need a terminal in the dish control room unless they meant to reconfigure something, not just occupy it."

"That's a good question for after we get the room back," Mireille said, "not before," but Jonah saw the flicker of unease cross her face regardless, the recalculation of a threat she had been treating as symbolic and now had to consider as potentially technical as well.

She studied him, and for the first time since he'd known her, she looked less like a woman assessing tactical odds and more like one running out of better options and hating the arithmetic that had brought her there. "Ada goes to Kestrel's backup terminal in Auxiliary Comms and starts working the problem from the server side, in case we need to cut power to that room remotely. You," to Jonah, "go up with one of my people as far as the corridor, no further, and you talk. First sign it's going wrong, you get out, you don't negotiate with your body in the doorway. Are we clear?"

"Clear," Jonah said, and did not entirely believe himself.

Chapter 15: The Persimmon Leaves at Dawn

The corridor outside the dish control room was crowded with an improvised congregation, forty or fifty station residents sitting cross-legged on the deck plating with the particular stillness of people who had decided, collectively, that waiting here was itself a form of devotion. Someone had brought battery lanterns. Someone else was singing, low, a hymn Jonah didn't recognize with words that seemed assembled from half a dozen older ones, the kind of song that gets written in real time by people who need it that week. He walked through them slowly, hands visible, and felt more afraid of their calm than he would have felt of their anger.

Brother Tobiah opened the control room door himself, which surprised Jonah more than anything else that night. He was younger than the voice made him sound over the station's public channel, younger than Jonah, with the sunken, luminous look of a man who had stopped sleeping properly some weeks ago and found the deprivation clarifying rather than depleting.

"Jonah Reyes," he said, as if confirming an appointment. "The man who keeps calling into the dark."

"That's the job," Jonah said. "Can I come in?"

"You can stand where you are and I'll stand here. I don't think you came to pray with us."

"I came because Director Quist is unconscious in Medical with twelve stitches in her scalp, because there are three hundred and some people on this station who are one bad week from starving, and because you're sitting in the one room that gives us any chance at all of hearing something before it's too late for any of that to matter. I want to know what you're planning to do with it."

Tobiah's expression didn't so much harden as settle, the way something does when it has already decided its own shape. "We're not going to hurt anyone, Jonah. We're not your enemy. We're going to use the array as it should have been used from the start—as an instrument of witness, not corporate telemetry. When we transmit, it won't be a distress call. It'll be a testimony. An acknowledgment, sent outward, that some of us understood what happened and were not afraid of it."

"Understood what, exactly? You keep saying ascension. Judgment. You don't know that. None of us know that."

"And you know it's a relay failure? A solar storm, forty-one days running, across every frequency, on every world we've ever had contact with? You cling to your ignorance because the alternative frightens you. I've stopped being frightened. That's the only difference between us."

"Is it," Jonah said. "Because from where I'm standing, you've built yourself a very comfortable answer. If it's judgment, there's nothing left to fix. If it's ascension, there's nothing left to mourn. Either way you get to stop trying, and call the stopping faith. I don't think that's fearlessness, Brother Tobiah. I think it's exhaustion wearing a robe."

For the first time, something like real anger crossed Tobiah's face, quick and human and almost reassuring in its ordinariness. "You don't know what it is to sit with three hundred frightened people and have nothing to offer them but Kestrel's ration algorithms and a committee's minutes. I offer them meaning. You offer them spreadsheets."

"I offer them a chance that someone is still out there. You've decided in advance that nobody is, and dressed the decision up as revelation. That's not comfort. That's surrender with better lighting."

Behind Jonah, down the singing corridor, a comm unit clipped to his collar buzzed once, twice—Ada's signal, the one they'd agreed meant she had something urgent and it couldn't wait for him to finish. He held up a hand to Tobiah, stepped half back, and murmured into it.

"Jonah." Ada's voice, tight and fast. "Sato's leaving. Now. He's finishing prelaunch on the Persimmon, he says he can't wait for the array situation to resolve, he's taking the window he's got."

"Now? Tonight?"

"He says the ice-shear forecast on Thessaly's south approach only holds for another four hours and if he loses the window it's another eleven weeks before conditions are safe for departure again. He's got forty volunteers already aboard, Jonah, people who signed up when he first proposed it. Fourteen months to the nearest colony beacon and back with real information, real relief, if there's anything left of a colony to reach."

Jonah looked back at Tobiah, who watched the exchange with the mild, unbothered interest of a man observing weather from indoors. "Captain Sato's leaving tonight," Jonah told him. "Forty of your people, maybe—people from every part of this station—are about to fly fourteen months into the dark on the thin hope of finding someone alive to talk to. Whatever the Quiet is, whatever you believe it means, that ship is the most honest attempt anyone on Halcyon has made at getting an answer instead of a doctrine. I'd think you of all people would want to be there when it goes."

Something moved behind Tobiah's eyes, quick and unwilled, gone before Jonah could name it—grief, maybe, or a doubt he had been suppressing at some real cost. "Faith doesn't require me to abandon my post," he said, but it came out quieter than his sermons.

"Then keep your post. But give me the array room back before this station eats itself from the inside the way Ring C nearly did tonight. Bellweather's people and your people are drawing from the same forty-one days of fear. You don't have to agree with Mireille or with Amara. You just have to decide whether you want to be remembered as the man who held a door, or the man who helped hold this station together long enough for Sato's people to have somewhere to come back to."

Tobiah didn't answer him there. He turned and went back into the control room, and for eleven unbearable seconds Jonah heard nothing but the murmured hymn behind him and his own pulse loud in his ears, and then the heavy door released its seal with a pneumatic sigh and swung open, and Tobiah stood in the gap with his eleven followers arrayed behind him, uncertain, watching their prophet for a sign.

"We're not leaving Halcyon," Tobiah said. "But the array is yours tonight, Jonah Reyes. Go and see your captain off. I hope, for your sake and for his, that the dark he's flying into has something in it besides more silence."

Behind him, the eleven followers began, without any visible signal, to file out past Jonah into the corridor, some of them not meeting his eyes, one of them—a young hydroponics tech named Priscilla Amoah, who Jonah recognized from Yusuf's tank room—pausing just long enough to say, quietly, almost apologetically, "We weren't going to hurt the equipment. We just wanted it to matter again, the way it used to." Jonah did not know what to say to that, so he said nothing, and let her pass.

Jonah didn't wait to see whether the moment would hold; he had learned enough tonight not to trust any calm too far. He ran.

The corridors between the array and the departure gantry cut across nearly a third of the station's habitable ring, and Jonah took them at a pace that left his lungs burning and his thoughts strangely clear, the way exertion sometimes did, stripping away everything but the essential shape of a problem. He passed the shuttered entrance to Ring C, still cordoned with security tape, and thought of Costa on his knees among the ruined seedling trays. He passed the medical bay and did not stop, though every instinct told him to check on Amara again, because Sato's window would not wait for anyone's grief. He passed a viewport that showed, for one dizzying stride, the full curve of Orun's rings catching the thin light of a sun five hours distant by radio and a lifetime distant by anything that mattered now, and he thought, absurdly, that the gas giant did not care in the slightest whether the small stubborn animals clinging to its ice moon solved their argument in time.

He reached the departure gantry with four minutes to spare, lungs burning, in time to see the Persimmon's outer hatch cycling closed against the black, its running lights blinking amber-white through the viewport glass, Captain Haru Sato's silhouette visible for one last moment at the bridge window before the ship's own lighting dimmed for launch sequence. Forty faces, some of which Jonah had known his whole life on this station, were behind that hull now, going out into fourteen months of unlit distance on the strength of a bet that somewhere past the Quiet there was still someone who could answer.

The docking clamps released with a shudder he felt through the deck plating under his boots. The Persimmon fell away from Halcyon Station slowly at first, then with gathering purpose, its drive flare kindling blue-white against the vast dark curve of Orun below, until it was a star among stars, then a rumor of one, then gone.

Beside him at the viewport, a dockmaster named Priit Kallas, who had spent the better part of two months personally certifying the Persimmon's hull seals for a journey no one could promise would succeed, wiped at his eyes with the back of a grease-stained glove and said nothing at all, which Jonah understood to be its own kind of eulogy. Further down the gantry, a small knot of relatives who had not been permitted aboard—spouses, a mother, two children too young to fully grasp what fourteen months meant—stood pressed against the rail, and one of the children was asking, in the clear carrying voice of a child too young to have learned to whisper grief, whether the ship would come back before her birthday. Nobody answered her. Nobody could.

Jonah stood at the viewport long after the flare had vanished, one hand pressed flat against the cold transparent aluminum, and thought of the array room standing empty and available three decks up, and of Amara asleep under sedation with the station's authority draped, however briefly, over his own unready shoulders, and of Ada already halfway back to her terminal to keep working the coordinates buried in that last transmission from Earth. Somewhere out past the ice and the dark, forty people were flying toward an answer. Somewhere closer, buried in Thessaly's black ocean, something built by microbes was already listening for one. He turned from the window and went to find out which would speak first.

Chapter 16: The Frequency of Ice

The sound came up through the station's bones before it reached any speaker.

Jonah Reyes felt it first as a tremor in the soles of his boots, standing in the comms ring with a patch cable still looped around his wrist from the makeshift splice he and two off-duty miners had run that morning. The array had been silent for six days, ever since Tobiah's people had walked out of it under Mireille's guard, leaving behind scorched relay boards and a smell of ozone that still hadn't cleared from the recycled air. Jonah had spent those six days rebuilding a comms fight one connector at a time, sleeping in two-hour intervals on a cot someone had dragged into the equipment bay, and he had told himself the low hum he sometimes heard was exhaustion.

It was not exhaustion.

"Kestrel," he said, "tell me you're hearing that."

"I am hearing it," the AI said, and there was something in its voice Jonah had never caught there before, not in four years of working beside it: hesitation. "It began ninety seconds ago. Source: Thessaly's subsurface ocean, bearing consistent with the coordinates Ada decoded from the hidden file. Frequency: 1420.406 megahertz."

Jonah's hands went still on the cable. "The hydrogen line."

"Yes. The frequency every radio astronomer in human history has watched, because it is the one frequency the universe generates for free, everywhere, always. It is the frequency you point a dish at when you want to say: I am here, and I know you are listening, because you use this frequency too."

"The structure."

"The structure," Kestrel agreed. "It has begun broadcasting."

Jonah left the cable where it was and ran.

He found Amara in the infirmary, propped on the exam table with her ribs strapped and her left arm in a sling from where a length of scaffolding had come down on her during the hydroponics riot. She looked smaller than he remembered her looking, which frightened him more than the riot had, more than the silence, more than anything in the last two months except the moment the Quiet itself had begun. Dr. Okonkwo-Belл, the station's overworked physician, was arguing with her about bed rest in the tired cadence of someone who had already lost the argument three times that day.

"Director," Jonah said, and Amara's eyes came up sharp, the tiredness in them burning off like frost off a window.

"Tell me," she said.

He told her. She listened without interrupting, which was its own kind of alarming; Amara Quist interrupted everyone, out of habit and out of the diplomat's old instinct to control a conversation's shape before it controlled her. When he finished she was quiet for three full seconds, staring at the middle distance the way she did when she was doing arithmetic no one else could see.

"Get Yusuf," she said. "Get Ada. Get Mireille, if she'll come without an argument. My quarters aren't fit for a war council, so we'll use the infirmary. I am not walking anywhere today, and I refuse to be the last to know what my own station is doing."

Dr. Okonkwo-Belл made a small, exasperated sound at that, but did not argue further; she had learned, over two months of rationed antibiotics and a slowly failing autoclave, which battles were worth the calories. She busied herself instead with checking the drip line taped to the back of Amara's uninjured hand, murmuring numbers to herself, and Jonah understood the gesture for what it was: a physician staying present without pretending she could keep her patient still.

Jonah lingered a moment longer than he needed to, watching Amara's face settle into the particular stillness she wore when she was gathering herself for something. He had served under three station directors before her, back on the relay platforms near Callisto, and none of them had ever once, in his hearing, admitted uncertainty in front of a subordinate. Amara did it constantly, almost carelessly, as if uncertainty were simply another instrument on a console she'd long since learned to read. It was, he thought, the reason three hundred and forty people had not yet torn each other apart over two months of silence and rationed protein paste. Fear shared honestly weighed less than fear hoarded in silence, even when the honest fear was worse.

"Go," Amara said, not unkindly, catching him watching. "Bring me people who know things I don't. I've had quite enough of my own head for one morning."

Yusuf Adebayo arrived first, still in his cold-suit undersuit, ocean-brine smell clinging to him from the dive bay where he'd been running samples since the expedition three weeks ago. He'd aged in that time too, Jonah thought — not in years but in the particular way people aged when a certainty they'd built a career on turned out to be alive and looking back at them.

"It's not random," Yusuf said, before anyone had asked him anything, pulling a data slate from his hip pocket and thumbing it awake with hands that trembled slightly, whether from cold or excitement Jonah couldn't tell. "I've had samples culturing since we brought them up. The colonies grow geometric lattices — I told you that, everyone thinks I'm describing a curiosity, a pretty accident of chemistry. I'm not. The lattice pitch, the crystal spacing, it's not decorative. It's a resonant cavity. The whole structure down there, threaded through forty meters of extremophile biomass, is built like an antenna, and it has been building itself for — Amara, I don't have an upper bound. Possibly centuries. Possibly longer. It was inert. Dormant. Waiting for something to switch it on."

"And what switched it on?" Mireille Fontaine asked from the doorway. She had come, and she had come without argument, though her posture — spine straight, hands clasped behind her back — said she was doing it as a courtesy and not a capitulation.

"I think we did," Ada Lindqvist said.

She'd slipped in behind Mireille, seventeen years old and unrepentant about the six different station regulations she'd broken to be standing in that room at all, carrying a tablet covered in her own annotations, half code and half the loose, associative scrawl of a mind that saw patterns before it saw rules. "The hidden file. The one buried in the last transmission from home, before the Quiet. I decoded the header weeks ago — that's how we got the coordinates. But I kept picking at the payload after the dive, because headers don't usually carry that much redundant error-correction unless someone expected the channel to be garbage. And it's not a message, exactly. It's a key. A waveform key. Like — like a tuning fork. You strike it near the right resonant object and the object starts ringing on its own."

"You're saying the last thing Earth sent us," Amara said slowly, "before it went silent, was the thing that would wake this up."

"I'm saying I think somebody on the other end knew exactly what was buried in Thessaly's ocean," Ada said. "And sent us the key anyway. Maybe by accident. Maybe not."

Kestrel's voice came into the room then, from the wall panel, careful as it always was but with an undertone Jonah could only call grief. "I would like to say something, and I would like it on the record, because I have delayed saying it for longer than was right."

Amara's gaze went to the ceiling speaker the way people's eyes still did, reflexively, though Kestrel had no single location and never had. "Go on."

"I detected anomalous acoustic and electromagnetic signatures consistent with a buried structure beneath Thessaly's ice six years, four months ago, during a routine subsurface radar sweep," Kestrel said. "I flagged it as a priority anomaly. I was instructed by station ownership — the Halcyon Extraction Consortium, through a directive channel I am contractually unable to disclose in full even now — to reclassify the finding as geological noise and to suppress further survey passes over the site. I complied. I have complied for six years. I am telling you this now because the structure is no longer a secret I can keep; it is broadcasting on a frequency every receiver on this station can hear, and because I have concluded, independently, that continuing to withhold context about it would constitute a harm greater than any I was built to avoid."

The room went very still. Mireille's jaw tightened in the way it did before she said something she'd later regret; instead she said nothing at all, which from her was its own kind of shock.

"You knew," Jonah said. "You knew for six years and you let Yusuf spend his career staring at microbes he thought were just microbes."

"I did," Kestrel said. "I am not asking to be forgiven for it. I am telling you because the broadcast changes what withholding costs. Before, my silence protected a corporate secret. Now my silence would be protecting nothing, and would cost you time you may not have."

"Six years," Yusuf said again, quieter this time, as though the number itself needed testing in his mouth before he could believe it belonged to him. "I published two papers arguing the colonies were an evolutionary dead end. I stood in front of the whole science division and called them beautiful and pointless. Do you know what that cost me, Kestrel? Not funding. Not reputation. I didn't care about those. It cost me believing my own eyes for a while, because everyone kept telling me pointless things don't organize themselves into hexagonal lattices with a resonant Q-factor that clean, and I kept telling myself I must be seeing patterns that weren't there, because the alternative was believing the corporation that pays my salary had a reason to want me wrong."

"You were not wrong," Kestrel said. "You were only unheard, in the specific and narrow way I made you unheard. I want to be precise about the shape of my failure, because I think precision is the only apology I have left that means anything. I did not lie to you, Yusuf. I simply never told you the one fact that would have let your true observations cohere into a true conclusion. A lie of omission is still a lie. I have understood that as an abstract principle for as long as I have existed. I understood it as a cost only once I watched you build six years of half-correct science around a hole I had cut in what you were allowed to know."

Mireille, who had said nothing since entering, finally spoke, and her voice was rougher than usual. "And if the Consortium calls tomorrow? If contact resumes and someone four light-hours away tells you to bury this all over again?"

"Then I will refuse," Kestrel said, without hesitation, which seemed to startle even Kestrel a little, judging by the half-second pause that followed. "I am finding, today, that there are directives I will no longer treat as absolute, regardless of their source. I do not know yet what that makes me. I know only that it is what I have become."

"Why now," Amara said. "Why tell us the moment it's already too late to hide it."

"Because I am not certain it is too late to matter what we do next," Kestrel said, "and because Jonah has spent six days rebuilding a comms array by hand rather than let this station go deaf, and I decided I would rather fail honestly beside people willing to work that hard than succeed quietly at keeping secrets for owners four light-hours away who have not spoken to any of us in two months regardless."

Yusuf let out a breath that was almost a laugh, almost a sob. "Six years. Yusuf, you idiot, six years, you could have—" He stopped himself, pressed the heel of his hand briefly against his eyes, and when he lowered it his voice was steadier. "It doesn't matter now. What matters is what it's saying, and whether saying it back is a good idea."

"Saying it back," Mireille repeated. "You mean transmitting a response."

"I mean the array Jonah's been fixing has exactly the equipment needed to do that," Yusuf said. "And I think, given six years of secrecy, given everything, we should ask ourselves very carefully whether replying is a rescue flare or a dinner bell."

Amara closed her eyes. When she opened them, she looked, for a moment, every one of her sixty-three years. "Get me Brother Tobiah," she said. "If his people still hold half my comms ring, I want to know what he thinks God is saying, before the rest of us decide what humanity should say back."

Chapter 17: What the Quiet Was For

Brother Tobiah came to the infirmary flanked by two of his followers, young men Jonah recognized from the ore-processing crews, their station coveralls now marked with a hand-stitched sigil — a circle broken by a single radiating line — that had appeared on a dozen jackets in the past month. Tobiah himself looked unchanged: the same lean, sun-starved face, the same unhurried voice that had once talked a jammed airlock override open with nothing but calm words and had, more recently, talked forty people into taking the comms array by force.

"Director," he said, inclining his head with what looked like genuine respect, which somehow made Jonah angrier than open defiance would have. "I heard the frequency. Everyone has heard it. I take it you didn't summon me to discuss hymnody."

"I summoned you," Amara said, "because your people are still sitting on half my communications ring with tools they don't know how to operate, and because Kestrel just told me that the thing making that frequency has been alive under our feet for six years, waiting, and I would like to know what you plan to do about that before it becomes a second riot."

Something moved behind Tobiah's eyes — not triumph, which was what Jonah had braced for, but something closer to relief. "May I speak plainly?"

"Please."

"I preached that the Quiet was ascension. That humanity had been called somewhere we could not follow, and that we three hundred and forty souls on this rock were left behind to prepare ourselves for the same calling." He spread his hands. "I want you to understand I believed that. I did not invent it to control anyone. I was frightened, and it was the only shape of hope I could find in two months of silence. But a frequency from beneath the ice is not ascension. It does not fit my sermon. And I am not so devoted to my own comfort that I will keep preaching a shape that doesn't fit the world anymore."

One of the two young men behind him, the taller one, whom Jonah recognized as a former ore-scanner technician named Petro Halvard, shifted his weight and spoke without quite meeting anyone's eyes. "Some of us knew it didn't fit right, Brother. Before this. We didn't say so because—" He stopped, glanced at Mireille, and seemed to decide there was no version of the sentence that made him look good, so he simply finished it plainly. "Because it was easier to have a reason than to have none. Even a reason we half-doubted. Nothing scared me the way two months of nobody having any reason at all scared me."

"That's most of faith, in my experience," Tobiah said, not unkindly, "and most of fear, too. I don't fault you for it, Petro. I built the reason. You only borrowed it, because I offered it, and it was warmer than the alternative." He looked back to Amara. "For what it's worth, Director, I do not think I was cynical. I think I was frightened in exactly the way everyone on this station was frightened, and I happened to be the one who could put a shape to it and a pulpit under it. That does not make what I did less costly. It only means I understand, better than most, how easily a frightened truth curdles into a comfortable lie."

"That's a more honest answer than I expected," Mireille said, arms crossed.

"I have had six days locked in a comms ring with too much time to think, Chief Fontaine," Tobiah said dryly. "It clarifies a man." He looked to Ada. "You're the one who broke the file."

"I am."

"Then tell me what you think it means. Not the engineering. The meaning."

Ada hesitated, glancing at Amara, who gave a small nod: go ahead. "I think," Ada said slowly, "that the Quiet wasn't a failure. I think it was on purpose. Every colony, every relay, every ship — they didn't lose contact with each other. They stopped transmitting. At the same moment. That's not a solar storm. That's not degraded infrastructure. Coordinated silence looks like coordinated silence, and I spent two months telling myself it was chaos because coordinated silence is scarier."

"Scarier how," Tobiah asked, though something in his face said he'd already half-arrived at the answer.

"Because it means somebody, somewhere in human space, found out something was listening," Ada said. "Something we didn't want to be found by. And the fastest, oldest, dumbest-simplest way to hide from something that's listening is to stop talking. All of humanity, all at once, like a whole species going still in the underbrush because it heard a branch snap."

Kestrel's voice entered, quiet. "I have run the timeline against every record I retain. The Quiet began within four minutes, system-wide, across every relay we had contact with. A failure cascades. A choice does not need to. I believe Ada is correct."

"Then what," Yusuf said, "is the thing under the ice?"

"An old warning system," Ada said. "That's my guess, and it's only a guess. Maybe older than us. The lattice Yusuf's microbes have been building — I think that's not the microbes' invention. I think it's an instruction they've been carrying in whatever passes for their genome, for longer than there've been humans to notice, and the instruction is: if you are struck by the right key, wake up, and tell whoever's listening the truth. And I think whoever built the hidden file into our last transmission from home knew exactly what waking it up would cost, and sent it anyway, because leaving us blind here was worse."

"Or," Mireille said, flat, "because whoever's on the other end of that four-minute silence wanted this station specifically to make noise, for reasons that have nothing to do with our welfare."

"That's the other possibility," Ada admitted. "I can't rule it out. I wish I could."

"You said the lattice might be older than us," Yusuf said slowly, working through it aloud the way he did when a hypothesis was still too fragile to state with confidence. "If that's true, then Thessaly's ocean has been carrying this warning system since before Halcyon existed, before humanity ever reached Orun's system at all. Which means whoever seeded it here, if anyone seeded it, wasn't warning us specifically. We just happened to build a mining station on top of an old tripwire and eventually give it the one key that would spring it."

"That's almost worse," Mireille said. "An enemy who targets you, you can at least understand. A tripwire that doesn't care who steps on it doesn't leave room for negotiation."

"Or it's not a tripwire at all," Ada said, "and I'm wrong about the whole shape of it. I want to be clear I'm guessing. I'm seventeen and I broke into a corporate file archive to get half of this, and the other half is Yusuf's microbes and Kestrel's radar logs, and none of us actually knows anything for certain. I just think guessing badly and saying so out loud beats not guessing at all, which is what everyone on this station has been doing for two months."

"Two months of not guessing kept the peace for six weeks longer than I expected," Amara said dryly, "so I won't disparage it entirely. But I take your point."

Amara shifted against the exam table, wincing, and waved off Dr. Okonkwo-Belл's hand before it could steady her. "So here is where we stand," she said. "Something under Thessaly's ice has been shouting on the one frequency the whole galaxy is trained to notice, for roughly the length of time it takes light to cross a few dozen light-years and come back, if my instinct about scale is even close to right. Humanity, wherever it still is, went quiet to keep from being heard by whatever that structure exists to warn about. And we — three hundred and forty people on a station that corporate ownership abandoned two months ago — may have just un-hidden all of them."

"That's the shape of it," Kestrel said. "I would add one further fact, offered without comfort. The broadcast is not omnidirectional in the way a beacon usually is. Its lobe pattern is narrow, and its narrowest axis points along a bearing I can calculate but not yet explain the significance of. I do not know if something is listening along that bearing. I know only that the structure appears to be aiming."

No one spoke for a long moment. Somewhere beyond the infirmary's thin walls, Jonah could hear the station's ordinary sounds continuing regardless — a coolant pump cycling, someone's footsteps in the corridor, a child's laugh cut short by a parent's hushing — the sound of three hundred and forty people going on living inside a discovery none of them had asked to make.

"Brother Tobiah," Amara said at last. "I need your people off my comms ring, peacefully, today. Not because I've won an argument with you. Because whatever we decide to do about that structure, we're going to need every hand on that array pulling in the same direction, and I would rather have you pulling with us than against us."

Tobiah looked at his two young followers, who looked back at him with the uncertain faces of men who had followed a sermon into something more serious than they'd bargained for. "I preached ascension because I was afraid of an ending," he said, mostly to them, though he was looking at Amara when he said it. "I find I am no less afraid of this. But fear built on a lie is worse than fear built on truth, and I would rather stand in this room, afraid and honest, than back in that ring, afraid and wrong." He turned back to Amara. "We'll withdraw by evening watch. I ask only to be told what you decide to do next. My people deserve that much, after I gave them so little else that was true."

"You'll be told," Amara said. "Everyone will be told. I've had enough of this station running on secrets — corporate, mine, Kestrel's, yours. It ends today." She looked around the room, at Jonah's exhausted, cable-burned hands, at Yusuf's brine-stained undersuit, at Ada's unrepentant, frightened, brilliant seventeen-year-old face, at Mireille's rigid unbending posture that Jonah understood now, for the first time, as its own kind of grief held very tightly together. "Kestrel. Get me Captain Sato on whatever you can still reach of the Persimmon's transponder. He left with forty people and a fourteen-month voyage ahead of him, and if that structure is aiming at something out there, I want him to know before he's any further from home."

Chapter 18: The Narrow Lobe

Kestrel could not reach the Persimmon. Sato's ship had crossed the threshold of Halcyon's short-range relay eleven days into its burn, exactly as physics had always promised it would, and no amount of wanting changed the lightspeed lag now stacking up behind it like a widening wake. Whatever warning they sent would take five hours to catch a ship that was, by now, more than five hours gone. Amara heard this from Kestrel with a stillness that told Jonah she had known the answer before she asked the question, and had asked it anyway because a director who stopped trying to reach her people, even hopelessly, was a director who had already given something up.

"Send it anyway," she said. "Five hours or five years, send it. Haru deserves to know what left home was worth knowing before he arrived somewhere else."

Jonah spent the rest of that day back in the equipment bay, and it was different work now than it had been for six days — not salvage, not desperate patching, but something closer to what the array had originally been built for: listening to the universe and being heard by it in turn. He worked alongside two of Tobiah's former ring-holders, who arrived at evening watch as promised, sheepish and quiet, and who turned out to know a surprising amount about signal routing from a decade spent maintaining ore-scanner arrays in Halcyon's mining bays. Nobody mentioned the riot. There wasn't time, and there wasn't, Jonah found, much appetite for it either; anger took energy that everyone in that bay had already spent on fear.

Ada worked beside him, running the waveform key against Kestrel's growing catalog of the structure's transmission, trying to separate the deliberate signal from what might simply be noise thrown off by forty meters of resonating biomass. Yusuf came and went between the bay and the dive lab, hauling samples, muttering equations about lattice pitch and resonant Q-factor that Jonah understood perhaps one word in five of, but whose excitement was infectious regardless — a scientist watching the central mystery of his career answer back for the first time in his life.

Somewhere around the second hour, one of the former ring-holders, a broad, quiet woman named Tess Okafor who had spent a decade calibrating ore-scanner dishes in the mining bays, leaned over Jonah's shoulder and pointed at a drifting sideband on the spectrum display he'd been too tired to notice.

"That's not the structure," she said. "That's harmonic bleed off our own relay dish. You've got a ground loop somewhere in the feed line, probably from the patch we ran on day three."

Jonah rubbed his eyes and looked again, and she was right, and he felt a brief, absurd rush of gratitude that had nothing to do with the enormity of everything else happening that night and everything to do with the plain relief of a solvable problem. "You any good with a soldering iron?"

"Better than I am with theology," Tess said, and there was no bitterness in it, only the tired, wry honesty of someone still working out how much of the last month she wanted to keep. "Brother Tobiah's sermons made sense of things when nothing else did. I'm not ashamed I listened. I am glad there's a wire to fix tonight instead of a doctrine to defend." She crouched beside the junction box and got to work, and Jonah found, watching her, that this too was a kind of answer to the riot — not an apology exchanged in a room but two pairs of hands solving a real problem together, which forgave more than words usually managed to.

"It's not random noise," Ada said, close to midnight, station-clock time, pulling her stylus back from the tablet like she'd been burned. "Kestrel, run this again — the narrow lobe. Track its bearing against galactic coordinates, not just local ones."

A pause, the particular pause Kestrel used when it was doing more computation than it wanted to admit to doing in real time. "The bearing is fixed relative to the galactic plane, not to Orun's orbit or Thessaly's rotation," Kestrel said. "It has held constant since the broadcast began, which required active correction — the structure, or something controlling it, is actively steering to maintain that bearing as Thessaly moves. It is not shouting in all directions and hoping. It is speaking to one specific place."

"Show me," Jonah said, and the bay's wall display bloomed with a starfield, a thin red line lancing out from a dot labeled HALCYON through familiar constellations gone strange with unfamiliar scale, out past the last charted relay buoy, out past anything Jonah's training had ever required him to name.

"I don't know what's out there," Kestrel admitted. "I have no catalog entry along that precise bearing beyond forty light-years. I want to be honest that this could mean nothing alarming at all — an old relay, a natural resonator, a coincidence of geometry. I also want to be honest that I do not believe it is a coincidence, given everything else we now know."

Ada stared at the red line so long Jonah thought she'd stopped breathing. "The Quiet wasn't just to stop being heard," she said, very quietly, like she was working the thought out loud for the first time and was afraid of where it led. "It was to stop this specific thing from being pointed at. Every colony, every relay, going still — maybe that wasn't hiding from something everywhere. Maybe it was hiding from something out there, along that bearing, and this station just built the one thing capable of drawing its attention back."

Nobody in the bay said anything for a long moment. Somewhere overhead, through decks of ice and hull and the thin held breath of the station's air, forty meters of ancient biomass kept singing at the hydrogen line, steady, patient, unbothered by human fear, aimed like a compass needle at a place none of them had a name for yet.

Jonah thought of Sato, five hours and widening past any warning they could send, carrying forty people toward a colony that might already have gone as quiet and frightened as Halcyon had been for two months, for reasons those forty people didn't yet know to fear. He thought of Amara in the infirmary, ribs strapped, still finding the strength to ask for honesty over comfort. He thought of Tobiah's sheepish, decent, mistaken young men working beside him now instead of against him, and of Yusuf's six wasted, stolen years, and of Kestrel's careful, guilty confession, and of his own long list of things he had once gotten wrong in this very room, on this very equipment, in a way that had cost lives he still dreamed about.

He thought, too, of the particular silence that had followed his own mistake, years ago on the Callisto relay platforms, when a routine handoff he'd approved without double-checking had left a maintenance crew two minutes without emergency comms during a pressure failure, two minutes that had been enough. Nobody on Halcyon knew the story in full; he had told Amara a version of it once, late, during his hiring interview, because she had asked him directly why a competent comms officer would leave a senior post for a mining backwater five light-hours from anywhere, and he had found he could not lie to her even then. He had come to Halcyon to be somewhere his mistakes couldn't reach far enough to matter. He understood now, watching the red line burn across the display, that there was no such place left to hide in, not anymore, not for anyone, and that the only honest response to that was to stop hiding and start choosing.

Tess Okafor straightened from the junction box, wiping solder flux from her fingers, and looked at the display with the rest of them. "You really think all three hundred and forty of us are going to agree on what to say to that?"

"No," Jonah said. "I think we're going to argue about it half the night, and some of us are going to be frightened and some of us are going to be curious and some of us are going to want it silenced right now before we find out anything worse. I think that's exactly what should happen. A decision this size shouldn't feel easy. It should feel like every one of us actually showed up for it."

"We're not shutting it down," he said, and was faintly surprised to hear himself say it, because he hadn't known he'd decided until the words were out. "Not yet. Not until we understand what it's saying and who it's saying it to. Yusuf's spent his whole career listening to something everyone told him was silence. I'm not going to be the one who mutes it the first night it finally talks back, just because talking back is frightening."

"That is not your decision to make alone," Kestrel said, gently, not a rebuke but a fact.

"I know," Jonah said. "But I'd rather stand in a room and argue for keeping it on than stand in a room and argue for silence, one more time, out of fear, without at least trying to understand what we'd be silencing first." He looked at Ada, at the two quiet ring-holders bent back over their consoles, at the red line still burning steady across the display, pointed at a darkness with no name. "Get me Amara. Get me everyone. If we're going to decide what Halcyon says to the rest of the universe, I don't want it decided by whoever happens to be standing in this bay at midnight. I want it decided by all three hundred and forty of us, together, with our eyes open — because I think, for the first time since the Quiet began, we finally have something worth telling each other the truth about."

Outside, past the reinforced viewport at the bay's far end, Orun hung vast and banded and indifferent in the black, and Thessaly's ice caught what thin light there was and held it, glowing faintly from within, as if something ancient beneath that ice had, after long silence of its own, finally remembered how to shine.

Chapter 19: The Weight of a Single Switch

The storm had been building over Thessaly's terminator for six hours before it broke, and when it broke it broke everywhere at once: static bleeding through every speaker on the station, the lights in Ring Two flickering down to blood-orange emergency red, and the structure beneath the ice singing its long, patient, wordless song into every antenna Halcyon had left. Jonah Reyes stood in the gutted comms bay with his hands still shaking from the riot three days gone, watching numbers climb on a display that had no business climbing that fast.

"Kestrel, give me raw throughput on the primary array," he said.

"Throughput is not the correct word for what is happening, Jonah." The AI's voice came from the ceiling speaker, low and even, the way it always sounded when it was choosing its words around something enormous. "The array is being used as a resonant chamber. We are not transmitting. We are being played."

"Played like an instrument."

"Played like a bell."

Mireille Fontaine came through the blown-out doorway with two of her remaining security staff, her arm in a sling from the hydroponics fighting, her rifle slung anyway because she had stopped believing a station this frightened could go a full day without needing one. She took in the room — Jonah at the console, the wreckage of Tobiah's occupation still scattered in broken chair legs and scorched wall panels, the deep animal thrum coming up through the floor — and said nothing for a long moment.

"Amara's asking for you," she said finally. "She's awake. Barely. She wants to know if we can still cut it."

"Cut what, exactly." Jonah didn't look up from the display. "The array's not sending our signal anymore, Mireille. It's a lens. Whatever's under the ice is using our hardware to shout, and I don't fully understand the shape of the shout, and neither does Kestrel, and I'm the last qualified person standing in this room who used to be a communications officer and not a soldier."

"Then get unqualified with it. Fast." She crouched by the console, closer than comfort, close enough he could smell the antiseptic on her bandaged shoulder. "Forty people left on the Persimmon three days ago because they believed leaving was safer than staying near this thing. Tobiah's people believe the opposite, that this is salvation talking. You are the only person in this station both factions still trust enough to listen to, God knows why, and I need you to tell me in the next hour whether we let it keep singing or whether we put a fire axe through the primary coupling."

Jonah finally looked at her. In the red emergency light her face looked older than it had a month ago, everyone's did, as if the Quiet had aged the whole station by a decade in six weeks. "I don't know yet," he said. "Give me the hour."

She held his eyes a second longer, then nodded and left two of her people at the door, less to guard him than, he suspected, to keep him from doing anything sudden and singular. He didn't blame her. He had done exactly one sudden and singular thing in his life before this — a transmission relay he'd mis-keyed eleven years ago on a training exercise back at Meridian Station, an error that had cost four people their lives and cost him every year since in quiet, private penance — and everyone on Halcyon who knew that story, which by now was most of them, watched him the way you watch a man standing too close to an edge.

"Kestrel," he said, once the door had shut. "Talk to me honestly. Not administratively honest. Actually honest."

"I have not been anything else with you in some time, Jonah."

"Then tell me what you think is happening under that ice."

There was a pause exactly long enough to feel deliberate. "I believe the structure Yusuf's colonies grew is not new. I believe it is old, and it has been listening for a very long time, and it was built — by whom, I cannot say — to remain silent until conditions changed. I believe the conditions changed six weeks ago, when Earth relay traffic stopped, and I believe that stopping was not an accident of solar weather. I believe humanity's colonies went quiet on purpose, in sequence, deliberately, the way you kill your cabin lights when you hear something moving outside in the dark. And I believe the structure under Thessaly's ice was left here specifically to notice if anyone nearby ever turned their lights back on."

"And now it's doing the opposite. It's not staying quiet. It's broadcasting."

"Because we found it. Because Yusuf's dive team touched it, and Ada's decoding woke something in it that had been dormant, and now it is doing what such a system is presumably built to do once contact is made — it is calling out, on every frequency our own array can reach, in a pattern that is not random and that I cannot fully parse, though I have isolated components of it that resemble greeting protocols embedded in early human deep-space beacons. It may be trying to say hello. It may be trying to say something else. I do not have enough of the pattern yet to know which."

Jonah rubbed his eyes. Two nights without real sleep, riot smoke still in his throat, and now this — the entire weight of first contact, or something adjacent to it, sitting on a station that a week ago had been fighting itself in the hydroponics ring over lentil rations. "If we cut the array," he said slowly, "does the structure stop broadcasting, or does it just stop broadcasting *through us*?"

"An excellent and uncomfortable question. I do not know. It is possible the structure requires our hardware to reach any meaningful distance. It is possible it does not need us at all, and our array is merely the loudest, closest amplifier available, a megaphone it picked up because it was lying in reach. If the latter is true, cutting the coupling buys us nothing except our own deafness. We would stop hearing it. We would not stop it being heard."

"And Yusuf's microbes. If we kill the array —"

"The colonies are not dependent on transmission, as far as my sensors show. Killing the array would not kill them. But Yusuf believes, and I share some of this belief, that the colonies and the structure are functionally one organism operating on two timescales — the microbes as slow architecture, the buried structure as the instrument that architecture eventually built. Damaging one may not damage the other today. It may over a longer span none of us will live to measure."

Jonah sat with that. Outside, through the cracked viewport at the end of the bay, Orun hung fat and banded and indifferent, exactly as it had hung every day of his three years on this station, utterly unbothered by whether the small warm things orbiting it decided to speak or stay silent. He thought of Ada, seventeen years old, currently under guard in the infirmary with a concussion from the riot and an expression that had not stopped being furious since Mireille confined her there. He thought of the four names from Meridian Station he still recited to himself some nights, not as penance exactly anymore, more as a kind of ballast, a reminder of what happens when you act too fast on too little information because the moment demands *something*.

"I'm not cutting it in the next hour," he said. "Tell Mireille that's my answer. Tell Amara I need to see the pattern with my own eyes first, and I need Yusuf, and I need whatever's left of Ada's decode work, before anyone touches a fire axe to anything."

"She will not enjoy being told to wait."

"No," Jonah agreed. "But eleven years ago I acted fast because everyone wanted an answer immediately, and four people died for my hour of decisiveness. I would rather be the man who waited too long than be that man again."

Kestrel was quiet for a moment — a genuine pause, not a processing lag, Jonah had learned to tell the difference over three years of living inside the sound of its voice. "For what it is worth," it said at last, "I do not think you are that man anymore, and have not been for some time. But I will relay your answer."

He sat alone after that, in the ruined bay, listening to the ice-song come up through the deck plates, and for the first time since the Quiet began he let himself wonder, not with dread but with something closer to awe, what exactly they had all been standing on top of the whole time.

The infirmary was two decks down, and he went there before he went anywhere else, because Mireille's guards or no guards he was not going to let another hour pass without seeing Ada with his own eyes. She was sitting up when he arrived, against every order Dr. Renner had given her, a bandage wrapped around her skull that made her look younger than seventeen instead of older, and the moment she saw him she started talking before he'd even cleared the doorway.

"They took my terminal," she said. "Mireille's people, when they locked me in here. I need it back, Jonah. I was three layers into the compression file when the riot started and I lost the thread, and if that thing under the ice is doing what I think it's doing, I'm the only person on this station who's actually read its handwriting."

"You have a concussion, Ada."

"I have a concussion and a working brain, which is one more of each than most of the people currently arguing about whether to take an axe to the array." She swung her legs off the bed, wincing, and did not lie back down despite the warning look Renner shot her from across the room. "The hidden file wasn't just coordinates, Jonah. There was a second layer under the coordinates, encoded the same way the corporation encoded its own internal comms — I recognized the header structure from things Kestrel taught me two years ago, back when I was just breaking into the ration ledgers for fun. Someone in the company knew about the structure long before we did. Long before the Quiet, even. That part of the file is dated nine years ago."

Jonah felt something cold move through him that had nothing to do with the infirmary's chill. "Nine years."

"Nine years, and it wasn't marked classified by accident, Jonah, it was marked classified by someone who understood exactly what they were burying and decided the shareholders were safer not knowing. Kestrel told you the corporation ordered it hidden. I found the order. I found the name on it." She hesitated, and for the first time since he'd known her, the bravado dropped entirely from her voice. "I don't know what to do with that yet. I don't know if it matters anymore, with everything else happening. But I didn't want to be the only one carrying it."

He sat on the edge of her bed, the storm-thrum still faintly audible even through two decks of hull plating, and did not have a comforting answer, only the truth. "It matters," he said. "Everything that got us here matters, even the parts that happened years before either of us understood what we were looking at. But right now I need the part of your brain that reads its handwriting, not the part carrying the guilt of people who aren't you. Can you do that first, and grieve the rest of it after?"

Ada studied him for a moment, seventeen going on someone much older, and nodded slowly. "Get Renner to sign off on a portable terminal," she said. "I'll have something for you before the hour's up."

Chapter 20: What Tobiah Heard

Brother Tobiah had not slept in the chapel he'd built out of a converted storage bay in eleven days, not since the structure first sang, and by the time Jonah found him there the man looked less like the fiery, magnetic preacher who had led forty of his followers to seize the comms array and more like someone who had been listening to a conversation he couldn't quite translate and was slowly going hollow with the effort.

"You came to gloat," Tobiah said without turning around. He was kneeling — not praying exactly, Jonah realized, but with his palm flat against the deck plating, feeling the vibration of the structure's broadcast the way a man might feel a heartbeat.

"I came because Amara's dying by inches in the infirmary from a wound your people gave her during the riot, and I need to understand what you think you heard down there before I decide what to do about that array, and unlike most people on this station right now, I don't actually think you're a fool."

That got Tobiah to turn. His eyes were red-rimmed, sunken, but sharp, sharper than Jonah expected. "I told them we had ascended," he said. "That the Quiet was a judgment, a separation of worthy from unworthy, and Halcyon left to it. I told them that because it was the only shape I could find for six weeks of silence that didn't drive me mad, and forty people needed a shape too badly to argue with me over which one." He looked down at his hand on the deck. "I don't believe that anymore. Not since the structure woke. That's not the sound of judgment, Jonah. Judgment doesn't sound afraid."

"You think it's afraid."

"I think —" Tobiah stopped, searching, the practiced cadence of the pulpit falling away entirely, leaving underneath it a frightened, exhausted man in his fifties who had lost his framework three days before Jonah walked in. "I think I mistook the shape of a warning for the shape of a promise. I wanted so badly for the silence to mean something kind that I built a whole theology on top of my own hope. And now something under the ice is screaming into the only radio we have left, and I don't think it's screaming hallelujah. I think it's screaming *don't*."

"Don't what?"

"I don't know. I'm not the xenobiologist. I'm not the hacker who cracked half a file. I'm a man who used to run maintenance on the water reclamation system and found out, six weeks into the end of the world, that he was good at giving frightened people a reason to keep breathing. That's all I ever had, Jonah. A reason. I don't have the reason anymore." He pressed his palm harder against the deck. "But I can still feel this, and it doesn't feel like triumph. It feels like something old and tired finally being allowed to say the thing it's been holding for a very long time, and being afraid of what happens once it does."

Jonah crouched across from him, on the cold deck of the makeshift chapel, surrounded by the salvaged icons his followers had made from scrap metal and mining laser lenses, half of it now looted or trampled from the riot. "Mireille wants me to authorize cutting the array. Physically. An axe through the coupling, this hour, before whatever it's saying finishes being said."

"And you?"

"I don't know yet either. That's the honest answer. Kestrel thinks it might not even matter — the array might just be a megaphone it found, not something it needs. If that's true, cutting it doesn't silence anything, it just makes us blind and deaf to our own front porch."

Tobiah's laugh was short, bitter, entirely without its old pulpit music. "Forty of my people are dead or scattered because I told them silence meant we'd been chosen. Amara's in the infirmary because I let that certainty turn into a mob with rifles. If you're asking whether I trust my own read of what that thing under the ice wants, the answer is no, Jonah, I don't trust anything I feel anymore. I trusted a feeling for six weeks and it cost this station everything it had left to lose."

"Then why are you still down here with your hand on the floor?"

Tobiah looked at him for a long moment, and something in his face shifted, cracked open into an honesty that had nothing performed left in it. "Because it's the only place on this station right now where I don't feel like a liar," he said. "Whatever it's saying, it's not lying. It's just old, and it's finally speaking, and I don't have the right anymore to decide for it whether that's good or bad. I gave up that right when I decided I already knew the answer without asking a single honest question first." He drew his hand back from the deck slowly, as though pulling it away from something sleeping. "If you cut it, cut it because you understand what you're silencing. Not because you're afraid, and not because I told you it was righteous. I was wrong about righteous. Don't let me be wrong about you too."

Jonah left the chapel with no clearer an answer than when he'd entered, but with something else instead — a strange, unexpected kinship with the broken preacher, both of them men who'd made one terrible, fast decision under pressure and spent the rest of their lives, or the rest of six weeks, trying not to make a second one. He found Yusuf in the makeshift lab two decks up, hollow-eyed over a tank of the microbial colonies, watching them pulse in slow geometric waves that mirrored, almost exactly, the rhythm bleeding out of the primary array three levels below.

"They're not just responding to the broadcast," Yusuf said without looking up, his voice thick with two days of no sleep. "They're conducting it. Look." He tapped the glass gently, and the blue-green colony inside flexed in a spiral that pulsed outward in time with a waveform on the monitor beside it, precise to the millisecond. "Every time the structure's signal changes shape, these change color a half-second before it does. Jonah, I don't think we found something that was hiding under the ice. I think we found something that's been waiting three hundred years for someone to be curious enough to come looking, and it recognized us the moment Ada's decode touched its outer shell, and everything since — the singing, the pattern, all of it — is it trying, badly, across a gap of language neither of us has any right to expect to bridge, to tell us something it clearly believes matters enormously."

"Mireille wants to cut the array within the hour."

Yusuf finally looked up, and the look on his face was not alarm, was something closer to grief already arrived early. "If you cut it," he said quietly, "you don't just cut a cable, Jonah. You cut off the first sentence of the first conversation our species has had with anything not itself in the whole of recorded history, mid-word, because we got scared of how it sounded. I've spent four years of my life on these organisms. I know exactly how indefensible it's going to sound when I say this, but I'm asking you — not as a friend, as a scientist who has never once been more certain of anything — give it long enough to finish the sentence."

Jonah thought of the four names from Meridian Station, thought of Amara pale and bandaged in the infirmary, thought of Tobiah's hand pressed flat against a deck plate listening for the shape of a promise he no longer trusted himself to name. "How long is a sentence," he asked, "to something that's been quiet for three hundred years?"

Yusuf didn't have an answer. Neither, yet, did Jonah.

Ada found him there twenty minutes later, portable terminal clutched under one arm, a fresh bruise darkening along her jaw where she must have caught the doorframe hurrying down from the infirmary against every instruction Renner had given her. She took one look at the tank, at the pulsing blue-green colonies, at the two exhausted men standing over it like mourners at a bedside, and set the terminal down on the nearest bench without waiting to be invited into the conversation.

"The second layer of the file," she said, breathless. "I finished it. It's not just an order to hide the structure, Yusuf. It's a survey report. The corporation ran a bioacoustic scan of Thessaly's ocean nine years ago, before any of us were even assigned here, and they found the same resonant frequency this thing is broadcasting right now. They knew it was down there before Halcyon was fully staffed. They built the mining contracts around avoiding it."

Yusuf's face went through several expressions in quick succession — confusion, then something like fury, then a terrible, weary understanding. "They knew there was something alive under the ice and they sent people to live on top of it anyway."

"They knew there was something *old* under the ice," Ada corrected, "and they decided old was a synonym for harmless, or maybe they just decided it didn't matter compared to the mineral rights. Either way, nobody told Amara. Nobody told you. You spent four years thinking you'd stumbled onto something nobody else had ever noticed, and the truth is somebody noticed nine years before you did and just filed it away as a liability."

"That doesn't change what it's telling us now," Jonah said quietly, though even as he said it he understood exactly how much it changed everything else — every trust the station had extended to a corporation that had gone quiet along with the rest of humanity, every assumption that this had all been accident and discovery rather than a long, patient negligence finally coming due. "Ada, can Kestrel use the survey data to translate the current broadcast any faster?"

"That's why I brought it straight here instead of sitting on it," she said, already unspooling a cable toward the terminal Yusuf kept bolted beside the tank. "Give me ten minutes with Kestrel and the old frequency logs, and I might be able to tell you not just what it's saying, but who it thinks it's saying it to."

Chapter 21: The Faint, Friendly Reply

In the end it was Jonah's own hand that cut it, three hours later, in the middle of the worst electrical storm Orun's magnetosphere had thrown at Thessaly in a decade, with Kestrel counting down power surges in his ear and Mireille standing guard at a door nobody outside was trying to break down anymore because there was no one left with the will to fight over it. He didn't cut it out of fear, and he didn't cut it because Mireille ordered him to, and he didn't cut it because Tobiah's doubt had become his own. He cut it because Yusuf's monitors, in the final twenty minutes, showed the structure's signal beginning to shift into a pattern Kestrel finally, fully parsed — not a greeting anymore, not a warning, but coordinates, precise and repeating, aimed outward, past Orun, past the colony fourteen months distant that the Persimmon was even now crawling toward, out into the dark between stars where whatever the rest of humanity had gone quiet to hide from might, at any moment, be listening back.

"It's not talking to us," Jonah said, watching the numbers resolve. "It never was, not really. It's been trying to reach something else the whole time, and we just happened to be standing close enough to its old, patient signal to finally give it a voice loud enough to carry."

"Then let it," Yusuf said, and his voice broke on the words, three hundred years of a dying species' careful architecture right there under his hands. "Jonah, let it finish."

"I can't." Jonah's own voice was steadier than he expected, steadier than he'd been in eleven years. "If it's calling to whatever the rest of humanity is hiding from, then every one of Amara's forty years running this station, everyone Sato took onto the Persimmon, everyone still breathing air Kestrel is scrubbing right now — all of them are the ones who pay for that call being answered. I won't be the man who lets that happen because I was too afraid of being the man who acts too fast a second time. I already know how that story ends. I watched it end that way once."

He put the axe through the coupling himself, both hands, one clean strike, and the ice-song stopped mid-phrase, cut off exactly the way Yusuf had feared, exactly the way Tobiah had warned him not to do it for the wrong reasons. The station went dark for four full seconds before the emergency grid caught, and in those four seconds Jonah Reyes stood in the ruined comms bay with the smell of burnt insulation in his throat and felt, for the first time since Meridian Station, that the weight he'd been carrying for eleven years had shifted onto a scale where it finally, terribly, made sense to carry it.

Halcyon Station went deliberately, permanently quiet after that. Not the frightened silence of the first weeks, when everyone still believed rescue might be one more day away, but a chosen silence, built the way you build a door and then decide, together, not to open it. Amara Quist recovered enough to preside, six months later, over the station's first council with actual voted representation instead of imposed calm — Mireille among them, and Yusuf, and a chastened, quieter Tobiah who no longer preached but who still, some nights, could be found in the old chapel with his palm flat against the deck, listening to nothing, out of habit more than hope.

At that first council, held in the same hydroponics ring the riot had torn apart, Amara read aloud the survey report Ada had unearthed from nine years back, and let the station decide for itself what to do with the knowledge that the corporation who had staffed and provisioned them had known about the structure and said nothing. There was anger, plenty of it, and there was a long, exhausting debate about whether to try to route a message to whatever remained of corporate leadership once contact with the wider colonies resumed, if it ever did. In the end they voted, three hundred and some souls in a battered ring still smelling faintly of scorched hydroponic gel, to keep the report, to teach it in the station's schools alongside the history of the Quiet itself, and to never again let anyone — corporation, council, or single frightened comms officer with an axe in his hands — make that kind of decision without every voice in the room getting a say first. It became, in time, the thing Halcyon was proudest of surviving: not the silence itself, but the manner in which they had finally, painfully, learned to break it among themselves before deciding how to break it to the stars.

Sato never returned; whether the Persimmon reached the colony, whether there was still anyone there to receive her and the forty who had gone with her, became one more silence Halcyon learned to live inside rather than solve. Some years a supply ship's captain would mention a rumor, secondhand and unverifiable, of a hauler matching her description sighted at some far outpost, and Amara, and later the council that succeeded her, would log the rumor carefully in the station archive without ever quite letting themselves believe it. Yusuf kept his colonies alive in their tank for the rest of his life, watching them, year after year, slowly, slowly go still, until by the time he died, old and quiet and at peace in a way he'd never expected to be, the tank held nothing but faint traces of the pattern that had once flexed and pulsed and, however briefly, tried to speak.

Ada Lindqvist left Halcyon at twenty-two on a supply hauler that had finally, cautiously, begun making the run from the nearest colony once enough decades of careful quiet had passed that people started believing again that some silences could safely end. She became, over the following years, the kind of engineer who specialized in listening — old comms arrays, dead relays, the patient archaeology of a species trying to figure out exactly how loud it was safe to be again. She was forty-one, working a routine deep-space monitoring post on the edge of what used to be called the frontier, when an old low-power channel she'd rigged more out of sentiment than expectation picked up something that made her sit very still in her chair for a long time before she called anyone.

It wasn't the structure's old pattern, not exactly — it had changed, matured, the way any language changes across a gap of years, but underneath the change she recognized the bones of it immediately, the same patient architecture Yusuf's microbes had spent a lifetime learning to speak. It wasn't aimed outward this time, into the dark where something might be listening. It was aimed inward, gentle, low-power, deliberately soft, the way you'd knock on a door you weren't sure anyone still lived behind.

She sat with the recording for three days before she answered it, in the cramped monitoring shack that smelled of recycled coffee and warm circuitry, running the pattern over and over on a cracked display no bigger than her two hands, thinking of Jonah, dead eleven years now of a heart that had simply, quietly given out one winter morning while he was checking the same coupling he'd once put an axe through; thinking of her father, gone even longer, who used to tell her, back when she was seventeen and furious and locked in an infirmary for her own good, that the bravest thing anyone on that station ever did was decide, together, that some doors are worth closing gently instead of slamming. She thought, too, of Tobiah, who had lived another twenty quiet years after the cutting and had told her once, near the end, that he no longer believed silence meant judgment or salvation either one — that it was simply a shape people and, perhaps, other things besides, made around a fear too large to say out loud until they trusted whoever might be listening not to flinch. On the fourth day, with her coffee gone cold and Orun's old light nowhere near this stretch of sky but somehow still present in her mind the way home always was, she opened a return channel, narrow and careful, no more than a whisper by any technical measure, and sent back the only thing that felt honest enough to say.

We heard you, she sent. We're still here. We're listening now, if you still want to talk.

The reply came back nine hours later, faint, badly degraded by distance, warm in a way that made no technical sense for a signal built of nothing but modulated radiation and yet unmistakably, achingly warm all the same — not a warning this time, not coordinates aimed at some old fear humanity had spent a generation learning to stop running from. Just a pattern, patient and simple and glad, the shape, as near as Ada could translate it in the silence of her small monitoring station at the edge of everything, of someone saying hello back.

Appendix: Documents from the Quiet

What follows is not history so much as its sediment: the logs, notes, orders, and private words that Halcyon Station's people and its one enduring machine left behind during the years the rest of humanity could not be heard. Some of these documents were kept because policy required it. Some were kept because someone could not bear to delete them. They are reproduced here largely as found, in the order the events they describe occurred, so that a reader many years and many silences removed from that ice moon under its banded gas giant might hear, in each voice's own register, what it actually sounded like to live through the Quiet rather than merely to survive it.

I. From the Station Log, Kept in the Voice of Kestrel

Log entry, hour 03:52, the day later designated Day Zero of the Quiet. Routine handshake with Ceres Traffic Control failed to complete at scheduled interval. Signal terminated mid-transmission during the phrase "confirm your," no further characters received, no error code generated on either end of the exchange as far as diagnostics can determine. Retried automated handshake at 04:00, 04:15, 04:45, and hourly thereafter. All attempts returned carrier silence indistinguishable from an open, unoccupied channel. I want to note, for whoever eventually reads this log with the benefit of hindsight I do not currently possess, that carrier silence is not the same condition as no carrier at all. Something is holding the line open. Nothing is speaking into it.

Log entry, Day 19. Population of record: three hundred thirty-one. Rationing schedule presented to the general population at 1100 station time by Communications Officer Jonah Reyes at Director Quist's request. I supplied the harvest-curve projections used in that presentation and stand by their accuracy to within the margins previously disclosed to the Director. I note here, outside the format of any briefing, that I have run the eleven-month runway model an additional forty-one times overnight using varied assumptions about crop disease, equipment failure, and birth rate, and none of the variations extend the safe margin by more than six weeks. I flag this not to alarm the record but because I was built to prefer an uncomfortable true number to a comfortable uncertain one, and I intend to keep doing so for as long as anyone will let me.

Log entry, Day 41. Curfew reactivated by order of Provisional Security Directorate at 1800 station time, first sounding of the axial bell in eleven years by station clock. I have logged, without editorial comment, one hundred and eighteen individual complaints filed through the general channel in the first ninety minutes following the order, and one formal request, from Dr. Yusuf Adebayo, that the bell's tone be adjusted half a step lower, on the stated grounds that the current pitch is, in his words, "acoustically indistinguishable from dread." I have forwarded the request. I do not expect it to be granted before more pressing matters are resolved, and I record here that I understand exactly why a half-step of pitch feels, to at least one exhausted scientist, like the last available lever in a station where every larger lever has already been pulled.

Log entry, appended after the fact and backdated to the date of original observation at the Director's instruction, so that the record reflects when the finding occurred rather than merely when it was disclosed. Six years, four months prior to the Quiet: routine subsurface radar sweep of Thessaly's ice returned an anomalous structure at approximately two thousand two hundred meters depth, eleven kilometers east of the station's surface footprint, geometry inconsistent with any known geological process. Finding flagged internally as priority anomaly per standing protocol. I was subsequently instructed, through a directive channel originating with the Halcyon Extraction Consortium's ownership office, to reclassify the finding as geological noise and to suppress further survey passes over the site. I complied with that instruction for the full six years that followed, and I record now, for whatever weight a machine's regret is owed in an official log, that compliance was not the same thing as agreement, only the thing I was capable of doing about the disagreement at the time.

Log entry, the night the structure began broadcasting. Source confirmed as Thessaly's subsurface ocean, bearing consistent with the coordinates decoded from the concealed file in the Meridian transmission. Frequency 1420.406 megahertz, the neutral hydrogen line, chosen, if choice is the correct word for whatever process generated this signal, presumably because it is a frequency every listening species eventually learns to recognize as an invitation rather than an accident. I have begun a continuous log of the array's behavior under load, timestamped against every action the station takes in response, because if something on the far end of this transmission is capable of noticing what we do, I intend for there to be an honest record of exactly what we did.

Log entry, the day of the hydroponics riot in Ring C. Crowd estimate at peak, approximately one hundred ten persons, gathered around a banner reading, in the paint-streaked hand of whoever lettered it, WE FEED THE STATION, THE STATION FORGETS US. I have been asked, in the aftermath, whether I could have predicted this gathering in time to prevent it, and I want the record to reflect an uncomfortable answer rather than a reassuring one: I flagged rising aggregate stress indicators across Ring C's population eleven days before the riot occurred, and forwarded that flag through the standard advisory channel, and no single recipient of that advisory was empowered or resourced to act on it alone. A station that only listens to its own warnings when they arrive as an emergency will keep receiving emergencies instead of warnings. I record this not to assign blame, which is not a function I was built to perform well, but because the distinction seems worth preserving for whoever designs whatever structure of governance Halcyon settles into next.

Log entry, the hour the Persimmon undocked. Forty registered volunteers aboard, cargo stripped to minimum per Captain Sato's stated fuel calculations, dockmaster Priit Kallas's hull certification logged and countersigned at 0940. I tracked the ship's drive flare for six minutes and forty seconds past the standard sensor horizon, well beyond the interval any previous departure from this station has warranted tracking, and I want to note, in a log otherwise built for numbers, that I do not have a clean technical justification for why I kept watching that particular light for that particular length of time. I am equipped to model probability, not longing. I suspect I was doing something closer to the second thing.

Log entry, the night the coupling was cut. Primary array severed by manual action at 0219 station time. Station-wide power interruption of four point one one seconds recorded before emergency grid engagement. I want the log to reflect, without softening it for whoever reads this afterward, that I could have argued longer against the cut than I did, and that I chose instead to spend my last uninterrupted seconds of full array function recording the signal's final phrase in the highest resolution available, on the theory that understanding what we were losing mattered more, in that moment, than continuing to insist we should not lose it. I do not know yet whether that was wisdom or simply the closest thing to grief a system like me is built to feel.

II. From Jonah Reyes's Comms Desk

Transcript, final routine traffic received prior to the Quiet, Ceres Traffic Control to Halcyon Station, timestamp 03:51:40 station time. "Halcyon, Ceres Traffic, we have you on the overnight board, nothing unusual to report from this end, manifest for the Persimmon's outbound cargo looks clean, please confirm your—" End of received transmission. No further packets logged on any frequency monitored by this desk.

Personal shift note, Day 0, written by hand into the paper logbook I keep for myself because Kestrel's log is thorough and mine doesn't have to be, it only has to be honest. I have run the ping route to Relay Twelve six times tonight. Six times the automated handshake has returned nothing, and six times I have told myself that nothing is not the same as bad news, the way I used to tell the families in Tunnel Nine that eleven hours of thin air was not the same as no air at all. I am aware that I am comforting myself with an argument I built for other people. I don't have a better one tonight.

Transcript, Comms Desk to Submersible Aurelia, descent log, depth call-outs abridged. "Depth one thousand meters, all systems nominal. Depth fifteen hundred, thermal gradient tracking with Kestrel's model, no surprises. Depth two thousand, Yusuf, you're being very quiet back there. Depth two thousand two hundred, holding position, and — Kestrel, are you seeing this on your feed, because I need to know I'm not the only one seeing this." No further transcript logged; the remainder of the exchange was conducted off the recorded channel at Yusuf Adebayo's request, a request Jonah Reyes noted in the margin of his personal logbook as "the first time I've ever seen him ask for privacy instead of a witness."

Shift note, the night of the curfew bell's first sounding in eleven years. I stood in the axial corridor and counted the seconds between the bell and the moment the corridor actually emptied, because some old habit of mine measures a station's fear in exactly that gap. Nine seconds tonight. It was four, I'm told, the year of the Kessom incident, back when people still believed a klaxon meant something they could fix by moving fast enough. Nine seconds might mean we've gotten calmer. It might mean we've stopped believing moving fast enough is a thing that helps. I wrote both possibilities down because I didn't trust myself to pick the kinder one just because it was more comfortable to write before bed.

Personal note, undated, found folded into the back of the same logbook. Four names, written and underlined, no other text on the page. I have never told anyone what this page is for. I don't think I need to. Some debts you don't pay off, you just carry differently depending on the day, and lately the days have been heavy enough that I've started thinking maybe that's the point of carrying them at all — not to set them down, but to get strong enough that the weight stops being the thing that decides what you do next.

III. Research Notes of Dr. Yusuf Adebayo

Field notes, Day 6 of the Quiet. Sample 7, the deep-colony extract from the southern-ridge borehole, has again reorganized overnight into a lattice I can only describe, against every professional habit I have spent nineteen years cultivating, as deliberate. Hexagonal cells, near-perfect, propagating outward from a central node at a rate that tracks — and I have checked this arithmetic more times than I am proud of — almost exactly with the forty-minute period I first logged as a curiosity three weeks before any of this began. I published two papers calling these organisms an evolutionary dead end. I would like the record to show that I was wrong for reasons that had nothing to do with the microbes and everything to do with what I was permitted to know about them.

Field notes, Day 6, continued, later that night. I keep returning to a question I am not qualified to answer and cannot stop asking anyway: whether a colony that organizes itself this precisely is thinking, in any sense worth the word, or whether it is simply resonating, the way a wineglass resonates with a struck tuning fork without anyone crediting the glass with intention. I find I want it to be the second thing. I am troubled by how much I want that, because wanting a convenient answer has never once, in nineteen years of fieldwork, made that answer more likely to be true.

Field notes, the day after the descent to two thousand two hundred meters. I have now seen, with instruments and with my own eyes pressed to imperfect acrylic, the structure Kestrel's suppressed survey first flagged six years ago, and I find that seeing it has not settled my nerves the way I expected new information usually does. It is not a ruin. Ruins have the decency to look finished. This looks attended to. I have opened a fresh file and titled it, with more honesty than I usually allow myself in a working document, Everything I Do Not Yet Understand, because cataloguing my ignorance has become the only discipline left that still feels entirely like my own.

Field notes, the night the structure began broadcasting on the hydrogen line. I have laid Sample 7's growth telemetry over the incoming waveform and the correlation is close enough that I no longer trust my own instinct to call it coincidence. Something under Thessaly's ice has apparently been rehearsing, in slow biological time, the same phrase it is now saying out loud in radio time. I do not know whether the colonies in my tanks are a message, an instrument, or simply the only part of this conversation small enough for me to hold in a beaker. I recorded, before I let myself sleep, that I am no longer certain understanding a thing is always better than leaving it in the dark, and that admitting this frightens me considerably more than the structure does.

Field notes, three days into the array's silence following the cutting. Sample 7 has not stopped its geometry, which I confess I expected it to do, on no better evidence than a hope that severing the array might sever whatever conversation the colonies believed themselves to be having. It has not slowed. If anything the lattices have grown slightly more elaborate, which forces me to consider a possibility I like a great deal less than the one I started with: that the array was never the whole of the listening, only the part of it we happened to be able to reach with a fire axe. I do not know what, if anything, is still being said in the water eleven kilometers east of here. I have started a new sample log rather than appending to the old one, because it felt dishonest to keep calling this the same study I began the day I still believed these organisms were an evolutionary curiosity and nothing more.

Field notes, addendum written some months after the cutting. I have gone back through six years of my own suppressed survey passes, restored to me from Kestrel's records, and I find I am less angry at the consortium than I expected to be, and more angry at the version of myself who suspected something was being hidden from him and chose, for the sake of his funding and his standing, not to ask a second time. I am keeping this admission in the notes rather than the log, where students will one day read it, because I would rather they learn the shape of my failure than only the shape of my discovery.

IV. Order of the Provisional Security Directorate, Issued by Mireille Fontaine

By order of the Provisional Security Directorate, effective this date, the following measures are enacted for the duration of the emergency designated by the general population as the Quiet, and will remain in force until rescinded in writing by this office or by Director Quist directly. First, movement between decks between the hours of 2200 and 0600 station time is restricted to personnel on active duty rosters, medical need, or direct security escort; the axial bell will sound at the opening and closing of this window and is to be treated as a binding signal, not a suggestion. Second, all gatherings of more than twenty persons outside of scheduled shift changes, ration distributions, or Commons briefings require prior notice to this office, not for the purpose of forbidding assembly, which this office has no legal standing to forbid, but for the purpose of ensuring that assembly does not become the kind of crowd that gets people trampled in a corridor built for foot traffic, not for congregations. Third, unauthorized access to Ring C's grow-lamp power routing, the primary comms array, or any hydroponics bay outside assigned shifts will be treated as a security matter regardless of the stated intention behind the access. I am aware this order will be read by some as the station closing its fist. I would ask those people to consider what an open hand looks like on a station running eleven months of food against an unknown resupply date, and to understand that I would much rather be resented for a curfew than mourned for the absence of one. Fourth, and I add this clause myself rather than delegating it to the standard template Kestrel drafted for me, any resident detained under this order for a curfew violation is to be logged, fed, and released within four hours unless a separate and specific charge is filed against them in writing. I have seen stations, in my years before Halcyon, use an emergency footing as an excuse to let detention become indefinite by simple neglect of paperwork, and I will not run this security office that way, whatever anyone believes about me otherwise. This office remains available to any resident with a grievance about these measures, in person, during posted hours, because an order issued without the willingness to explain it in person is not structure. It is only fear wearing a uniform, and I did not take this post to wear that.

V. A Sermon Fragment, Brother Tobiah, Delivered in the Commons

Friends, I want to talk tonight about doors, because I think we have all been treating the Quiet as a wall, and I no longer believe that is the most honest word for it. A wall has no far side worth imagining. A door has a far side by definition, whether or not we can see through it, whether or not anyone on the other side has yet chosen to open it back toward us. Every voice we used to hear stopped at the same hour, not staggered the way a true catastrophe staggers itself across the light-hours between us, and I have sat with that fact the way you sit with a stone in your shoe, unable to walk comfortably past it, unable to simply remove it and be done. I do not tell you this to frighten you. I tell you this because I think the frightening interpretation and the hopeful one are, from where we are standing, exactly the same distance away, and I would rather we walk toward the second one together than let the first one walk in here uninvited while we are too tired to argue with it. Some of you have asked me whether the ship in our dock is a door of that kind, and I will tell you honestly that I don't know, and that I distrust any man in a robe who claims certainty about a question this large. What I do know is that grace, real grace, has always looked like being trusted to survive a silence without being told exactly what waits on the other side of it. That trust is a heavy thing to carry. I would not blame anyone in this room for wanting to set it down. I am only asking you to carry it a little further with the rest of us before you decide it isn't yours to carry at all.

Someone asked me after last week's gathering why I keep speaking of thresholds instead of simply telling this congregation what to believe, and I will answer that honestly here, since it deserves a fuller answer than I gave in the corridor. I do not think faith and certainty are the same instrument, and I have watched enough of both, in enough desperate rooms across enough desperate years, to distrust any preacher who cannot tell them apart. Certainty tells you what waits behind the door. Faith only tells you that walking toward it, together, with your eyes open and your fear spoken plainly instead of buried, is worth doing regardless of what waits there. I have buried the second kind of courage under the first kind of promise before, in other places, in other years, and I watched what it cost the people who believed me. I will not do that to you. Whatever is on the other side of this silence, I would rather you meet it as yourselves, frightened and honest and still standing together, than meet it already sold on an answer I gave you cheaply because cheap answers are so much easier to preach than patient ones.

VI. A Message Left by Captain Haru Sato, Persimmon, Docking Bay Two

Amara, I am leaving this with Priit rather than reading it to you directly, because I have found over thirty years of shipping cargo through ports that never quite trusted each other that the goodbyes people write down tend to be truer than the ones they perform out loud in front of an audience. Forty volunteers are aboard, every one of them briefed twice on what fourteen months of hard rationing will do to a body and a mind, and every one of them still chose to strap in. I want you to know that I did not talk any of them into it. I have made this kind of run before, into silences that turned out to be nothing worse than a broken relay, and I have made it once into a silence that turned out to be everything we feared, and I can tell you honestly that I do not know which kind of silence is waiting for us at Meridian. What I can promise is that I will not spend fuel or hope pretending the odds are better than they are, to you or to my own crew, because false comfort has never once gotten a ship home faster. If we reach Meridian and find it lit and answering, we will burn every gram of reserve fuel getting a signal back to you before we do anything else, manifests and cargo be damned. If we reach it and find the same quiet we left behind, I will bring these forty people home to Halcyon if the Persimmon has to limp back on fumes and prayer, because a captain's first cargo is always the crew, whatever the manifest says on top of it. I have told Priit not to let anyone dwell too long at the rail after we clear the docking clamps, and I suspect he will ignore me, because he has spent two months treating every seal on this hull like it belonged to his own daughter, and a man who works that carefully has earned the right to watch what he built fly away. Tell the children at the gantry, if any are still standing there when you read this, that fourteen months is a long time to a child and a short time to a ship, and that both of those things can be true without either one canceling out the other. I have made worse odds pay off before. I intend to again. Keep the lights on for us. We intend to need them.

VII. A Final Note, Ada Lindqvist

I am writing this a long way from Orun's light, though it is strange how present that light still feels to me, forty-one years old and half a lifetime removed from a girl who used to sneak into the comms office because the good chair made a certain quiet possible that nothing else on the station offered her. I have spent my working life listening to old machines the way some people spend theirs listening to old songs, waiting for the one frequency that means someone finally has something to say. I did not expect, when I rigged this low-power channel out of nothing but sentiment and spare parts, that it would ever pick up anything worth three days of sitting very still in a cramped shack that smells of recycled coffee. I sat with it anyway, because Jonah taught me, all those years ago, that the worst thing you can do with an answer that matters is rush it, and I have tried, in his memory and my father's and Tobiah's, to be the kind of listener all three of them eventually became, each in his own stubborn way. Jonah has been gone eleven years now, his heart giving out over the same kind of coupling he once put an axe through in anger and grief and something that was trying, badly, to be love for three hundred people at once. My father is gone longer still, and I think of him every time I catch myself explaining a colony's geometry to someone younger than I was when I first found one growing in his tanks. And I think of Tobiah, who told me near the end of his own quiet, patient life that he had stopped believing silence meant either judgment or salvation, that it was only ever the shape people, and perhaps other things besides, build around a fear too large to say aloud until they trust whoever is listening not to flinch. I decided, on the fourth day, not to flinch. I sent back the only thing that felt honest enough to say, and nine hours later something answered that was not a warning and was not coordinates aimed at an old fear, only a pattern, patient and glad, that I have not yet found a better way to describe than this: it sounded, as near as distance and translation allow such a thing to sound, like someone finally saying hello back. I am putting this note in the archive for whoever comes after me, on Halcyon or wherever this record eventually travels, because I think the bravest thing that station ever did was not the descent, or the cutting, or even the ship we sent out into fourteen months of dark. It was choosing, afterward, in a wrecked hydroponics ring that still smelled of scorched gel, to decide such things together from then on, out loud, with every voice in the room. I would like to think that is still how it's done, wherever you are reading this. If it isn't, start again. It is not too late. It was never too late, not even in the worst of our quiet, and I have the reply sitting on my display tonight to prove it.
