The Lighthouse at Vesta Gap

For eleven years, Oriel Dace kept the only lighthouse in the asteroid belt. It was not a tower of stone but a spinning drum of nickel-iron, hollowed out by miners long dead, fitted with a beacon that pulsed a slow green heartbeat across ten thousand kilometers of tumbling rock. Freighters threading the Vesta Gap watched for that pulse the way sailors once watched for fire on a headland.

Oriel's days were measured in maintenance. She polished the emitter lenses, patched micrometeor pits, argued with the station's aging mind, a gentle system she called Wick, about whether the coffee rations were truly running low or whether Wick had simply grown stingy. At night, if the drum's rotation could be said to have nights, she listened to the pilots' chatter drifting in on the open channel: complaints about cargo, jokes about their captains, the occasional quiet thank-you when they passed safely through.

Then, one cycle, the chatter stopped.

No freighters came. The channel hissed with empty static. Wick ran diagnostics on every antenna and found nothing broken. Oriel kept the beacon burning anyway, because that was the job, and because a lighthouse that goes dark is only a rock.

On the fortieth day of silence, a ship appeared that matched no registry. It was small, scarred, and moving far too slowly, its engines coughing like a sick animal. Oriel hailed it again and again. At last a child's voice answered, thin and frightened. The ship carried nine refugees from a mining colony on Hygiea, where a reactor failure had forced an evacuation. Their navigation computer had died somewhere past Pallas. They had crossed the belt by following one thing: a green light, blinking steadily, somewhere ahead in the dark.

Oriel talked them through the final approach herself, calling out each drifting boulder by name, her voice hoarse by the time their hull kissed her docking clamp. That night, nine strangers slept wrapped in thermal blankets beside her beacon's warm housing, and Wick, without being asked, unlocked the reserve coffee.

The freighters eventually returned; the silence had been a solar storm, nothing more. But Oriel never again wondered whether anyone was watching her light. She knew now that a beacon is not kept for the ships you see. It is kept for the one you never expect, the one steering by faith alone through the long, cold dark.
